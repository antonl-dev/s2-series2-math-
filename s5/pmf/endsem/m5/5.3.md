## 1. Concept and Definition of Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

The **Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)**—also known as the **Average Rate of Return** or **Return on Investment (ROI) method**—is a **traditional (non-discounting)** capital budgeting technique that measures a project's annual profitability as a percentage of its capital investment.

* **Core Formula Idea:**  
  ARR compares the **Average Annual Accounting Profit After Tax (PAT)** earned over the project's life to the **Investment** required.
* **Crucial Distinction:** Unlike Payback Period, NPV, IRR, and BCR (which all use **Cash Flows**), ARR uses **Accounting Net Profit (after deducting Depreciation and Taxes)**.

## 2. ARR Formulas and Key Variations

In practice and university examinations, ARR is computed using one of two standard denominators (**Average Investment** is the most widely accepted convention, though **Initial Investment** is also used):

### Method 1: ARR Based on Average Investment *(Most Standard)*
$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\text{Average Annual Profit After Tax (PAT)}}{\text{Average Investment}} \times 100\%$$
Where:
$$\text{Average Annual PAT} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n} \text{PAT}_t}{n}$$
$$\text{Average Investment} = \frac{\text{Initial Investment } (I_0) + \text{Salvage Value } (S)}{2} + \text{Additional Working Capital (if any)}$$
*(If Salvage Value = 0 and no working capital is specified: $\text{Average Investment} = \dfrac{I_0}{2}$.)*

### Method 2: ARR Based on Initial Investment
$$\text{ARR} = \frac{\text{Average Annual Profit After Tax (PAT)}}{\text{Initial Investment } (I_0)} \times 100\%$$

*(Exam Tip: If an exam problem does not specify which denominator to use, state the **Average Investment** formula clearly—or compute **both** in 2 extra lines for full safety!)*

### How to Derive PAT When Cash Flows / PBDT Are Given
If a problem gives **Profit Before Depreciation & Tax (PBDT)** or **Cash Flow After Tax (CFAT)** instead of PAT:
1. $\text{Annual Straight-Line Depreciation } (D) = \dfrac{\text{Initial Cost} - \text{Salvage Value}}{\text{Life } (n)}$
2. If **PBDT** is given: $\quad \text{PBT} = \text{PBDT} - D \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{PAT} = \text{PBT} \times (1 - \text{Tax Rate})$
3. If **CFAT** is given: $\quad \text{PAT} = \text{CFAT} - D$

## 3. Decision Rules for ARR

1. **Independent Project (Accept / Reject):**
   * **Accept** the project if $\text{ARR} \ge \text{Minimum Required Rate of Return (Target / Hurdle Rate)}$.
   * **Reject** the project if $\text{ARR} < \text{Target Rate}$.
2. **Mutually Exclusive Projects:**
   * Rank projects in descending order and select the project with the **highest ARR** (provided it exceeds the minimum target rate).

---

## 4. Advantages and Disadvantages of ARR

### Advantages
1. **Simple and Familiar:** Easy to calculate directly from standard accounting profit-and-loss statements; easily understood by executives and investors.
2. **Considers Entire Project Lifespan:** Unlike Payback Period, ARR includes profits from **every year** of the project's economic life.
3. **Measures Profitability Directly:** Focuses on net accounting return rather than merely how fast cash is recovered.

### Disadvantages
1. **Ignores Time Value of Money:** Treats ₹1,00,000 profit earned in Year 5 identically to ₹1,00,000 earned in Year 1.
2. **Uses Accounting Profit Instead of Cash Flow:** Accounting profits are distorted by depreciation methods (straight-line vs. WDV) and non-cash accrual rules; cash is what actually pays bills and loans.
3. **Ignores Project Scale & Reinvestment:** A ₹1 lakh project with 25% ARR looks better than a ₹1 crore project with 20% ARR, even though the latter creates far greater wealth.
4. **Multiple Formulas Cause Ambiguity:** Using Initial Investment vs. Average Investment produces different ARR percentages (often double!).

## 5. Worked Numerical Example

**Problem:** An engineering firm plans to buy a CNC machine costing **Rs. 2,00,000** with an economic life of **4 years** and a salvage value of **Rs. 20,000** (straight-line depreciation). Tax rate is **30%**. Expected Profit Before Depreciation and Tax (PBDT) is:
* Year 1: Rs. 85,000 | Year 2: Rs. 95,000 | Year 3: Rs. 1,05,000 | Year 4: Rs. 75,000

Calculate the **Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)** on Average Investment and Initial Investment.

**Solution:**
* **Step 1 — Annual Depreciation:**
  $$D = \frac{2,00,000 - 20,000}{4} = \frac{1,80,000}{4} = \text{Rs.\; } 45,000/\text{year}$$

* **Step 2 — Calculate PAT for Each Year:**

| Year | PBDT (Rs.) | Less: Dep (Rs.) | Profit Before Tax (PBT) | Less: Tax @ 30% | Profit After Tax (PAT) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 1 | 85,000 | 45,000 | 40,000 | 12,000 | **28,000** |
| 2 | 95,000 | 45,000 | 50,000 | 15,000 | **35,000** |
| 3 | 1,05,000 | 45,000 | 60,000 | 18,000 | **42,000** |
| 4 | 75,000 | 45,000 | 30,000 | 9,000 | **21,000** |
| **Total** | | | | | **1,26,000** |

* **Step 3 — Average Annual PAT & Average Investment:**
  $$\text{Average Annual PAT} = \frac{1,26,000}{4} = \mathbf{\text{Rs.\; } 31,500}$$
  $$\text{Average Investment} = \frac{2,00,000 + 20,000}{2} = \mathbf{\text{Rs.\; } 1,10,000}$$

* **Step 4 — Compute ARR:**
  $$\text{ARR (on Average Investment)} = \frac{31,500}{1,10,000} \times 100 = \mathbf{28.64\%}$$
  $$\text{ARR (on Initial Investment)} = \frac{31,500}{2,00,000} \times 100 = \mathbf{15.75\%}$$

**Answer:**

#### Model Question 1 (2 Marks – Part A Style)
**Source:** *Self-Created (Syllabus Topic 5.3 – ARR vs. Payback Period)*  
**Question:** State two differences between Payback Period and Accounting Rate of Return (ARR).

**Answer:**  
1. **Cash Flow vs. Accounting Profit:** Payback Period uses **Cash Flows After Tax (CFAT)**, whereas ARR uses **Accounting Profit After Tax (PAT)** (after deducting depreciation).
2. **Project Horizon:** Payback ignores cash flows *after* the investment recovery year, whereas ARR considers profits across the **entire economic life** of the project.

#### Model Question 2 (5 Marks – Part B Style)
**Source:** *Self-Created (Syllabus Topic 5.3 – Merits, Demerits & Short Numerical)*  
**Question:** Discuss the merits and demerits of the Accounting Rate of Return (ARR) method. A company plans to invest **Rs. 3,00,000** in a project with zero salvage value, generating Profit After Tax (PAT) of **Rs. 30,000, Rs. 45,000, and Rs. 60,000** over 3 years. Calculate ARR on Average Investment.

**Answer:**

**1. Merits & Demerits of ARR (3 Marks):**
* **Merits:** Simple to compute from accounting statements; accounts for profits over the entire project lifespan; measures profitability directly rather than mere payback speed.
* **Demerits:** Completely ignores the **Time Value of Money**; relies on accounting profits (affected by depreciation rules) rather than actual cash flows; ignores project size and timing of cash inflows.

**2. ARR Calculation (2 Marks):**
* $\text{Total PAT} = 30,000 + 45,000 + 60,000 = \text{Rs.\; } 1,35,000$
* $\text{Average Annual PAT} = \dfrac{1,35,000}{3} = \text{Rs.\; } 45,000$
* $\text{Average Investment} = \dfrac{3,00,000 + 0}{2} = \text{Rs.\; } 1,50,000$
$$\text{ARR} = \frac{45,000}{1,50,000} \times 100 = \mathbf{30\%}$$

#### Model Question 3 (8 Marks – Part B University Exam Style)
**Source:** *Self-Created (Syllabus Topic 5.3 – Mutually Exclusive Comparison Numerical + Theory)*  
**Question:** Explain the Accounting Rate of Return (ARR) method, its decision criteria, and limitations. Two mutually exclusive projects **X** and **Y** require an initial investment of **Rs. 1,00,000** each (life = 3 years, no salvage value, straight-line depreciation, no tax). Their **Cash Inflows (before depreciation)** are:
* **Project X:** Year 1 = Rs. 50,000; Year 2 = Rs. 50,000; Year 3 = Rs. 50,000
* **Project Y:** Year 1 = Rs. 30,000; Year 2 = Rs. 60,000; Year 3 = Rs. 75,000

Evaluate both projects using the ARR method (on Average Investment) if the company's minimum required return is **25%**.

**Answer:**

**1. Theory, Decision Criteria & Limitations (3 Marks):**
* **Concept:** ARR is a non-discounting ratio measuring average annual accounting profit (PAT) as a percentage of Average Investment $\left(\frac{I_0 + S}{2}\right)$ or Initial Investment.
* **Decision Criteria:** Accept projects with $\text{ARR} \ge \text{Minimum Target Rate}$; among mutually exclusive alternatives, select the one with the **highest ARR**.
* **Limitations:** Ignores Time Value of Money (early vs. late profits treated equally), depends on accounting depreciation methods rather than real cash flow, and ignores scale differences.

**2. Numerical Evaluation (5 Marks):**
* **Annual Depreciation (for both X and Y):**  
  $$D = \frac{1,00,000 - 0}{3} = \text{Rs.\; } 33,333.33/\text{year}$$
* **Average Investment (for both X and Y):**  
  $$\text{Average Investment} = \frac{1,00,000 + 0}{2} = \text{Rs.\; } 50,000$$

* **Project X:**
  * Total Cash Inflows $= 50,000 + 50,000 + 50,000 = \text{Rs.\; } 1,50,000$
  * Total Depreciation over 3 years $= \text{Rs.\; } 1,00,000$
  * Total Profit (PAT) $= 1,50,000 - 1,00,000 = \text{Rs.\; } 50,000$
  * Average Annual Profit $= \dfrac{50,000}{3} = \text{Rs.\; } 16,666.67$
  $$\text{ARR}_X = \frac{16,666.67}{50,000} \times 100 = \mathbf{33.33\%}$$

* **Project Y:**
  * Total Cash Inflows $= 30,000 + 60,000 + 75,000 = \text{Rs.\; } 1,65,000$
  * Total Depreciation over 3 years $= \text{Rs.\; } 1,00,000$
  * Total Profit (PAT) $= 1,65,000 - 1,00,000 = \text{Rs.\; } 65,000$
  * Average Annual Profit $= \dfrac{65,000}{3} = \text{Rs.\; } 21,666.67$
  $$\text{ARR}_Y = \frac{21,666.67}{50,000} \times 100 = \mathbf{43.33\%}$$

* **Recommendation:** Both projects exceed the 25% hurdle rate, and since $\text{ARR}_Y (43.33\%) > \text{ARR}_X (33.33\%)$, **Project Y** should be selected under the ARR criterion. *(Note: Even though X receives cash earlier, ARR ignores the Time Value of Money.)*
