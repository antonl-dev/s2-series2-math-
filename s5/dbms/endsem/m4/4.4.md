## PART A: Third Normal Form (3NF)

### 1. Why 2NF is Still Not Enough (The Problem of Transitive Dependency)
In Module 4.3, we learned that **2NF** eliminates **Partial Dependencies** (where a non-key column depends on *part* of a composite Primary Key).

**But what if a table has a Single-Column Primary Key (so it is 100% in 2NF), yet still has redundancy?**
Look at this `EMPLOYEE_DEPT` table where $\underline{\text{ENO}}$ alone is the Primary Key:

$$\text{EMPLOYEE\_DEPT} (\underline{\text{ENO}}, \;\text{EName}, \;\text{Salary}, \;\text{DNO}, \;\text{DName}, \;\text{DLocation})$$

| ENO (PK) | EName | Salary | DNO | DName | DLocation | Notice the Problem! |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **101** | Adhil | 60000 | **10** | EEE | Kollam | `'10, EEE, Kollam'` repeated! |
| **102** | Rahul | 30000 | **10** | EEE | Kollam | `'10, EEE, Kollam'` repeated! |
| **103** | Meera | 50000 | **20** | CSE | Kochi | |

* **Is this table in 1NF?** **Yes** (every cell is atomic).
* **Is this table in 2NF?** **Yes!** Because the Primary Key `ENO` is a **single attribute**, there cannot be any Partial Dependency!
* **Then why is `'10, EEE, Kollam'` still duplicated across rows?**
  Because a **Non-Prime Attribute (`DNO`)** is determining other **Non-Prime Attributes (`DName`, `DLocation`)**!

📸 **[Click here to view Third Normal Form (3NF) Transitive Dependency Diagrams on Google Images](https://www.google.com/search?q=third+normal+form+3NF+transitive+dependency+in+dbms+diagram&udm=2)**

```text
VISUALIZING THE TRANSITIVE DEPENDENCY THAT VIOLATES 3NF:

  [ ENO (Primary Key) ] ------> [ DNO (Non-Prime) ] ------> [ DName, DLocation (Non-Prime) ]
           \                                                            ^
            \__________________________________________________________/
               Transitive Dependency: ENO -> DNO -> {DName, DLocation} ❌ (Violates 3NF!)
```

---

### 2. Two Equivalent Definitions of Third Normal Form (3NF)

*(In the exam, always write **BOTH** definitions—the informal Transitive Dependency definition AND the formal Superkey/Prime-Attribute definition!)*

#### Definition 1 (Based on Transitive Dependency):
> *"A relation schema $R$ is in **Third Normal Form (3NF)** if and only if:*
> 1. *It is already in **Second Normal Form (2NF)**, **AND***
> 2. ***No non-prime attribute in $R$ is transitively dependent on the Primary Key (or any Candidate Key)**.*
> *(In simple words: **A non-key column cannot determine another non-key column!**)"*

#### Definition 2 (Formal Textbook Test for Any FD $X \rightarrow A$):
> *"A relation schema $R$ is in **3NF** if, for every non-trivial Functional Dependency **$X \rightarrow A$** that holds in $R$, **at least ONE** of the following two conditions holds:*
> * **Condition (a):** The left-hand side **$X$ is a Superkey** of $R$, **OR**
> * **Condition (b):** The right-hand side **$A$ is a Prime Attribute** (i.e., $A$ is part of some Candidate Key of $R$)."*

---

### 3. Step-by-Step Conversion of a Relation into 3NF

Let's convert our `EMPLOYEE_DEPT` relation above into **3NF**:

#### Step 1: Identify the Candidate Key and Functional Dependencies
* **Relation:** $\text{EMPLOYEE\_DEPT} (\underline{\text{ENO}}, \;\text{EName}, \;\text{Salary}, \;\text{DNO}, \;\text{DName}, \;\text{DLocation})$
* **Primary Key:** `ENO`
  * **Prime Attribute:** `ENO`
  * **Non-Prime Attributes:** `EName`, `Salary`, `DNO`, `DName`, `DLocation`
* **Functional Dependencies:**
  1. $\text{FD1: } \text{ENO} \rightarrow \{\text{EName}, \text{Salary}, \text{DNO}\}$ ✅ *(LHS `ENO` is the Primary Key / Superkey)*
  2. $\text{FD2: } \text{DNO} \rightarrow \{\text{DName}, \text{DLocation}\}$ ❌ *(**Violates 3NF!** Because `DNO` is NOT a Superkey, and `DName`, `DLocation` are NOT Prime Attributes! Hence, `DName` and `DLocation` are **transitively dependent** on `ENO` via `DNO`!)*

---

#### Step 2: How to Convert (Decompose) into 3NF!

> **The Mechanical Recipe to Convert Any 2NF Table into 3NF:**
> *"For each Transitive Dependency $X \rightarrow Y \rightarrow Z$ (where non-key $Y$ determines non-key $Z$), **pull the transitively dependent attribute(s) $Z$ out** of the original table and place them into a **new separate table** along with their determinant $Y$ as the Primary Key: **$R_{\text{new}}(\underline{Y}, Z)$**! Keep $Y$ inside the original table as a **Foreign Key**."*

Applying this rule to `EMPLOYEE_DEPT`, we decompose it into **two 3NF relations**:

##### 1. Relation 1: `EMPLOYEE` (Retains `DNO` as a Foreign Key, removes `DName` & `DLocation`)
$$\text{EMPLOYEE} (\underline{\text{ENO}}, \;\text{EName}, \;\text{Salary}, \;\text{DNO (FK)})$$
| ENO (PK) | EName | Salary | DNO (FK) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **101** | Adhil | 60000 | 10 |
| **102** | Rahul | 30000 | 10 |
| **103** | Meera | 50000 | 20 |

##### 2. Relation 2: `DEPARTMENT` (Created for the Transitive Dependency $\text{DNO} \rightarrow \{\text{DName}, \text{DLocation}\}$)
$$\text{DEPARTMENT} (\underline{\text{DNO}}, \;\text{DName}, \;\text{DLocation})$$
| DNO (PK) | DName | DLocation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **10** | EEE | Kollam |
| **20** | CSE | Kochi |

✅ **Why Both Tables Are Now in 3NF:**
* In `EMPLOYEE`, all non-prime attributes (`EName`, `Salary`, `DNO`) depend directly and solely on the Primary Key `ENO`.
* In `DEPARTMENT`, all non-prime attributes (`DName`, `DLocation`) depend directly and solely on the Primary Key `DNO`.
* Zero Transitive Dependencies remain!

## PART B: Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF / 3.5NF)

### 1. Why Was BCNF Needed? (The "Loophole" in 3NF!)
Look back at **Definition 2 of 3NF** above:
* 3NF says an FD $X \rightarrow A$ is allowed if **(a) $X$ is a Superkey**, **OR (b) $A$ is a Prime Attribute** (part of some Candidate Key).
* Notice **Condition (b)**! It is a "loophole" that allows a **Non-Superkey ($X$)** to determine a **Prime Attribute ($A$)**!
* When a table has **overlapping composite Candidate Keys**, this loophole allows a Non-Key attribute to determine part of a Candidate Key, which still causes redundancy and anomalies!

To close this loophole completely, **R. F. Boyce and E. F. Codd (1974)** introduced a stricter version of 3NF called **Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)**.

---

### 2. Definition of Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)

> *"A relation schema $R$ is in **Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)** if and only if it is in 1NF and, for **EVERY** non-trivial Functional Dependency **$X \rightarrow Y$** that holds in $R$:*
> $$\textbf{The Left-Hand Side (Determinant) } X \textbf{ is a SUPERKEY of } R!"$$

* **In Simple Words (The Easiest Definition in All of DBMS!):**
  **"In BCNF, the Left-Hand Side of EVERY arrow ($X \rightarrow Y$) MUST be a Superkey / Candidate Key! No exceptions!"**

📸 **[Click here to view BCNF vs 3NF Diagrams and Examples on Google Images](https://www.google.com/search?q=BCNF+boyce+codd+normal+form+in+dbms+with+example+diagram&udm=2)**

```text
COMPARING THE RULE FOR EVERY NON-TRIVIAL FD ( X -> A ):

  * In 3NF:   LHS (X) is a Superkey   OR   RHS (A) is a Prime Attribute.
  * In BCNF:  LHS (X) MUST be a Superkey!  (Even if A is a Prime Attribute!)
```
*(Therefore, **every relation in BCNF is automatically in 3NF**, **2NF**, and **1NF**, but a relation in 3NF is **not necessarily** in BCNF!)*

---

### 3. Classic Worked Example: A Relation That is in 3NF, But NOT in BCNF!

Consider a university relation **`STUDENT_ADVISOR`** with 3 attributes:
$$\text{STUDENT\_ADVISOR} (\text{Student\_ID}, \;\text{Subject}, \;\text{Professor})$$

Suppose the university follows these two rules (Functional Dependencies):
1. **$\text{FD1: } \{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Subject}\} \rightarrow \text{Professor}$**
   *(For a given subject, a student is guided by only ONE professor).*
2. **$\text{FD2: } \text{Professor} \rightarrow \text{Subject}$**
   *(Each professor teaches/guides ONLY ONE specific subject, e.g., `'Dr. Rao'` only teaches `'AI'`, though `'AI'` can be taught by multiple professors).*

**Sample Table (`STUDENT_ADVISOR` – In 3NF, but Violates BCNF!):**
| Student_ID | Subject | Professor | Notice the Redundancy! |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **S01** | **AI** | **Dr. Rao** | `'Dr. Rao -> AI'` repeated! |
| **S02** | **AI** | **Dr. Rao** | `'Dr. Rao -> AI'` repeated! |
| **S01** | **DBMS** | **Dr. Smith** | |

---

#### Step 1: Find the Candidate Keys of `STUDENT_ADVISOR`
Let's compute closures:
* $\{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Subject}\}^+ = \{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Subject}, \text{Professor}\}$ *(using FD1)* $\implies$ **Candidate Key 1 = `(Student_ID, Subject)`**
* $\{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Professor}\}^+ = \{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Professor}, \text{Subject}\}$ *(using FD2)* $\implies$ **Candidate Key 2 = `(Student_ID, Professor)`**
* Notice that **all 3 attributes** (`Student_ID`, `Subject`, `Professor`) belong to a Candidate Key—so **all 3 are Prime Attributes**!

#### Step 2: Check 3NF vs. BCNF on `FD2` ($\text{Professor} \rightarrow \text{Subject}$)
* **Is it in 3NF?** **YES!** Because the RHS (`Subject`) is a **Prime Attribute** (part of Candidate Key 1), so it passes Condition (b) of 3NF!
* **Is it in BCNF?** **NO!** Look at the LHS of `FD2`: **`Professor`**. Is `Professor` alone a Superkey of the table? **NO!** ($\{\text{Professor}\}^+ = \{\text{Professor}, \text{Subject}\}$, which does not determine `Student_ID`).
* Because the determinant `Professor` is **NOT a Superkey**, `FD2` ($\text{Professor} \rightarrow \text{Subject}$) **VIOLATES BCNF**!

---

#### Step 3: How to Convert (Decompose) ANY Table into BCNF!

> **The Universal BCNF Decomposition Rule:**
> *"Whenever an FD **$X \rightarrow Y$** violates BCNF (because $X$ is not a Superkey of $R$), decompose relation $R$ into **two relations**:*
> 1. **$R_1(\underline{X}, Y)$** *(Contains the violating FD, with $X$ as its Primary Key)*
> 2. **$R_2(R - Y)$** *(Contains all original attributes of $R$ EXCEPT $Y$, keeping $X$ as a Foreign Key!)*"

Applying this exact rule to the violating FD **$\text{Professor} \rightarrow \text{Subject}$** ($X = \text{Professor}$, $Y = \text{Subject}$):

##### 1. Relation $R_1(\underline{X}, Y)$: `PROFESSOR_SUBJECT`
$$\text{PROFESSOR\_SUBJECT} (\underline{\text{Professor}}, \;\text{Subject})$$
| Professor (PK) | Subject |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Dr. Rao** | AI |
| **Dr. Smith** | DBMS |

##### 2. Relation $R_2(R - Y)$: `STUDENT_PROFESSOR` (Remove `Subject` from original table!)
$$\text{STUDENT\_PROFESSOR} (\underline{\text{Student\_ID}, \text{Professor (FK)}})$$
| Student_ID (PK) | Professor (PK, FK) |
| :--- | :--- |
| **S01** | Dr. Rao |
| **S02** | Dr. Rao |
| **S01** | Dr. Smith |

✅ **Now BOTH $R_1$ and $R_2$ are in BCNF** (and `'Dr. Rao -> AI'` is stored only once)!

## PART C: Side-by-Side Comparison: 3NF vs. BCNF

*(Memorize this table—it is one of the most frequently asked 3–4 mark questions in Module 4!)*

| Feature | Third Normal Form (3NF) | Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Formal Rule for Every Non-Trivial FD $X \rightarrow A$** | Either **$X$ is a Superkey** **OR** **$A$ is a Prime Attribute** (part of a Candidate Key). | **$X$ MUST be a Superkey** (No exception for Prime Attributes!). |
| **2. Strength / Strictness** | **Less strict** than BCNF (every BCNF table is in 3NF, but not every 3NF table is in BCNF). | **Stricter (Stronger)** than 3NF (also called $3.5\text{NF}$). |
| **3. Redundancy Elimination** | Eliminates almost all redundancy, **except** when a non-key determines a prime attribute. | Eliminates **100% of redundancy** caused by Functional Dependencies. |
| **4. Lossless-Join Property** | **Always guaranteed** (100% lossless decomposition can always be achieved). | **Always guaranteed** (100% lossless decomposition can always be achieved). |
| **5. Dependency Preservation** | **Always guaranteed!** Every original FD can always be preserved in 3NF. | **NOT always guaranteed!** Decomposing into BCNF may break/lose some FDs across tables. |

## PART D: Master Cheat-Sheet – How to Find the "Highest Normal Form" of Any Relation in 60 Seconds!

In exams, you may be given a relation $R(A, B, C, D)$ and a set of FDs $F$, and asked: **"Find the highest normal form of $R$ (1NF, 2NF, 3NF, or BCNF)."**

Follow this **exact 4-Step Checklist**:

```text
STEP 1: Find ALL Candidate Keys of R (using Attribute Closure X^+).
        Identify:
        - Prime Attributes (in any Candidate Key)
        - Non-Prime Attributes (NOT in any Candidate Key)
                                |
                                v
STEP 2: Assume R is at least in 1NF (atomic values).
        Now check EVERY non-trivial FD ( X -> A ) against the ladder below:
```

| Normal Form Check | Question to Ask for Every Non-Trivial FD ($X \rightarrow A$) | If YES for All FDs | If NO for Any FD |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Test BCNF First!** | **Is the LHS ($X$) a Superkey?** | 🎉 Highest NF is **BCNF**! *(Done!)* | Not in BCNF $\rightarrow$ Check 3NF next. |
| **2. Test 3NF Next!** | For any FD that failed BCNF, **is the RHS ($A$) a Prime Attribute?** | 🎉 Highest NF is **3NF**! *(Done!)* | Not in 3NF $\rightarrow$ Check 2NF next. |
| **3. Test 2NF Next!** | Does any FD have a **Partial Dependency** ($\text{Proper Subset of CK} \rightarrow \text{Non-Prime Attribute}$)? | If **NO** Partial Dependency exists $\rightarrow$ 🎉 Highest NF is **2NF**! | If a Partial Dependency exists $\rightarrow$ 🎉 Highest NF is **1NF**! |

---

### Quick Worked Examples of Finding Highest Normal Form:

#### Example 1: $R(A, B, C, D)$ with $F = \{AB \rightarrow C, \;\; B \rightarrow D\}$
1. **Candidate Key:** $AB$ *(since neither $A$ nor $B$ is on any RHS, and $\{AB\}^+ = \{A, B, C, D\}$)*.
   * **Prime:** $\{A, B\}$, **Non-Prime:** $\{C, D\}$.
2. **Check FDs:**
   * Look at $B \rightarrow D$: $B$ is **part of Candidate Key $AB$**, and $D$ is **Non-Prime**.
   * That's a **Partial Dependency**!
3. **Result:** It violates 2NF, so its **Highest Normal Form is 1NF**!

#### Example 2: $R(A, B, C)$ with $F = \{A \rightarrow B, \;\; B \rightarrow C\}$
1. **Candidate Key:** $A$ *(since $\{A\}^+ = \{A, B, C\}$)*.
   * **Prime:** $\{A\}$, **Non-Prime:** $\{B, C\}$.
2. **Check FDs:**
   * Since the Candidate Key $A$ is a single attribute, there is **no Partial Dependency** $\implies$ **Passes 2NF!**
   * Look at $B \rightarrow C$: Non-Prime $B$ determines Non-Prime $C$ (**Transitive Dependency**!). Neither is $B$ a Superkey nor is $C$ Prime $\implies$ **Fails 3NF!**
3. **Result:** Its **Highest Normal Form is 2NF**!

#### Example 3: $R(A, B, C)$ with $F = \{AB \rightarrow C, \;\; C \rightarrow B\}$
1. **Candidate Keys:** $AB$ and $AC$ *(since $\{AB\}^+ = \{A, B, C\}$ and $\{AC\}^+ = \{A, B, C\}$)*.
   * **Prime:** $\{A, B, C\}$ (all 3 are Prime!), **Non-Prime:** None!
2. **Check FDs:**
   * Look at $C \rightarrow B$: Is LHS ($C$) a Superkey? **No** ($\{C\}^+ = \{B, C\}$) $\implies$ **Fails BCNF!**
   * Is RHS ($B$) a Prime Attribute? **Yes!** ($B$ is part of Candidate Key $AB$) $\implies$ **Passes 3NF!**
3. **Result:** Its **Highest Normal Form is 3NF**!

## PART E: Model Exam Questions & Full Word-for-Word Answers for 4.4

### Model Question 1 [4.5 Marks]:
> *"Explain Third Normal Form (3NF). Consider a relation containing transitive dependency and convert it into 3NF."*

#### ✅ Model Exam Answer:

**1. Definition of Third Normal Form (3NF):**
* A relation schema $R$ is in **Third Normal Form (3NF)** if:
  1. It is already in **Second Normal Form (2NF)**, and
  2. **No non-prime attribute of $R$ is transitively dependent on the Primary Key (or any Candidate Key).**
* Formally, for every non-trivial functional dependency $X \rightarrow A$ in $R$, either **$X$ is a Superkey** of $R$, or **$A$ is a Prime Attribute** (part of a Candidate Key).

**2. Relation Containing Transitive Dependency:**
Consider the relation **`EMPLOYEE_DEPT`** in 2NF:
$$\text{EMPLOYEE\_DEPT} (\underline{\text{ENO}}, \;\text{EName}, \;\text{Salary}, \;\text{DNO}, \;\text{DName})$$
* **Primary Key:** `ENO` (Single attribute, so the relation is already in 2NF with no partial dependencies).
* **Prime Attribute:** `ENO`
* **Non-Prime Attributes:** `EName`, `Salary`, `DNO`, `DName`

**Functional Dependencies:**
1. $\text{ENO} \rightarrow \{\text{EName}, \text{Salary}, \text{DNO}\}$ *(Direct dependency on Primary Key `ENO`)*.
2. $\text{DNO} \rightarrow \text{DName}$ *(Non-prime attribute `DNO` determines non-prime attribute `DName`)*.

Since $\text{ENO} \rightarrow \text{DNO}$ and $\text{DNO} \rightarrow \text{DName}$, the non-prime attribute `DName` is **transitively dependent** on the Primary Key `ENO` through `DNO`. Therefore, `EMPLOYEE_DEPT` violates **3NF**.

**3. Conversion (Decomposition) into 3NF:**
To convert `EMPLOYEE_DEPT` into 3NF, we remove the transitively dependent attribute `DName` and place it into a new relation with its determinant `DNO` as the Primary Key, while keeping `DNO` as a Foreign Key in the original relation:

* **Relation 1 (`EMPLOYEE`):**
  $$\text{EMPLOYEE} (\underline{\text{ENO}}, \;\text{EName}, \;\text{Salary}, \;\text{DNO (FK)})$$
* **Relation 2 (`DEPARTMENT`):**
  $$\text{DEPARTMENT} (\underline{\text{DNO}}, \;\text{DName})$$

Now, in both relations, the determinant of every functional dependency is the Primary Key (Superkey), and no transitive dependencies exist. Hence, both relations are in **3NF**.

### Model Question 2 [4 Marks]:
> *"Explain Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF) with a suitable example. How does it differ from 3NF?"*

#### ✅ Model Exam Answer:

**1. Definition of Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF):**
* A relation schema $R$ is in **Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF)** if it is in 1NF and, for **every non-trivial Functional Dependency $X \rightarrow Y$** that holds on $R$, **the determinant $X$ is a Superkey of $R$**.
* Unlike 3NF, BCNF does not allow any exception where a non-superkey determines a prime attribute.

**2. Suitable Example (In 3NF, but Violating BCNF) & Conversion:**
Consider the relation $\text{R}(A, B, C)$ representing `STUDENT_ADVISOR(Student_ID, Subject, Professor)` with FDs:
1. $AB \rightarrow C$ (`{Student_ID, Subject} -> Professor`)
2. $C \rightarrow B$ (`Professor -> Subject`)

* **Candidate Keys:** $AB$ and $AC$. Thus, all three attributes $A, B, C$ are **Prime Attributes**.
* **Why it is in 3NF:** In $C \rightarrow B$, the RHS ($B$) is a Prime Attribute, so 3NF is satisfied.
* **Why it violates BCNF:** In $C \rightarrow B$, the determinant $C$ (`Professor`) is **not a Superkey** of $R$.
* **Conversion to BCNF:** Decompose $R(A, B, C)$ on the violating FD $C \rightarrow B$ into two BCNF relations:
  1. $R_1(\underline{C}, B)$ $\rightarrow$ `PROF_SUBJECT(Professor, Subject)`
  2. $R_2(\underline{A, C})$ $\rightarrow$ `STUDENT_PROF(Student_ID, Professor)`

**3. Difference Between 3NF and BCNF:**
* In **3NF**, for $X \rightarrow A$, either $X$ is a Superkey **or $A$ is a Prime Attribute**. In **BCNF**, **$X$ must always be a Superkey**.
* **3NF** always guarantees both **Lossless Join** and **Dependency Preservation**, whereas **BCNF** guarantees a **Lossless Join** but does **not always preserve all functional dependencies**.

---

### 🎉 Module 4 Complete! Quick 10-Second Summary of All 4 Normal Forms:
* **1NF:** Every cell is **atomic** (1 single value per cell).
* **2NF:** 1NF + **NO Partial Dependency** (No $\text{Part of Key} \rightarrow \text{Non-Key}$).
* **3NF:** 2NF + **NO Transitive Dependency** (No $\text{Non-Key} \rightarrow \text{Non-Key}$; for $X \rightarrow A$, $X$ is Superkey OR $A$ is Prime).
* **BCNF:** For **every** $X \rightarrow Y$, **$X$ MUST be a Superkey!**
