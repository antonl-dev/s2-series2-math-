# 2.7 Process Scheduling: Priority Scheduling and Round Robin Scheduling

---

## 1. Priority Scheduling

### How it Works
In Priority Scheduling, a priority number (typically an integer) is associated with each process. 
- **Rule:** The CPU is allocated to the process with the **highest priority** (the process in the ready queue with the highest priority number).
- *Note on numbering:* Different operating systems use different conventions. In some systems (like UNIX/Linux), a **lower number indicates a higher priority** (e.g., 0 is the highest priority, 10 is lower). In other systems, a higher number indicates higher priority. (Always check the problem statement, but usually, small integer = high priority).

### Preemptive vs. Non-Preemptive
Priority scheduling can be either preemptive or non-preemptive:
- **Preemptive:** If a new process arrives at the ready queue with a higher priority than the currently running process, the CPU preempts the current process and gives the CPU to the newly arrived process.
- **Non-Preemptive:** The new process is simply put at the head of the ready queue, and the current process is allowed to finish its CPU burst.

*(Note: SJF is actually a special case of priority scheduling where the priority is the inverse of the next CPU burst time—the smaller the burst, the higher the priority).*

### The Major Problem: Starvation (Indefinite Blocking)
- **The Issue:** A major problem with priority scheduling algorithms is **starvation**. In a heavily loaded system, a steady stream of incoming high-priority processes can prevent a low-priority process from *ever* getting the CPU. 
- *Historical Fact:* When the IBM 7094 at MIT was shut down in 1973, researchers found a low-priority process that had been waiting in the ready queue since 1967!

### The Solution: Aging
- **Aging** is a technique used to overcome starvation. 
- **How it works:** The OS gradually increases the priority of processes that wait in the system for a long time. For example, if a priority ranges from 127 (low) to 0 (high), the OS might decrease the priority number of a waiting process by 1 every 15 minutes. Eventually, even the lowest-priority process will age into the highest-priority process and execute.

## 2. Round Robin (RR) Scheduling

### How it Works
The Round Robin algorithm is designed specifically for **time-sharing** systems. It is essentially FCFS, but with **preemption** added to switch between processes.
- **Time Quantum / Time Slice ($q$):** A small unit of time is defined, generally ranging from 10 to 100 milliseconds. 
- **Rule:** The ready queue is treated as a circular queue. The CPU scheduler goes around the ready queue, allocating the CPU to each process for a time interval of up to 1 time quantum.
- **The Process:**
  1. The scheduler picks the first process from the ready queue.
  2. It sets a hardware timer to interrupt after 1 time quantum.
  3. **Scenario A (Burst $< q$):** If the process has a CPU burst less than the time quantum, it finishes early, releases the CPU voluntarily, and the scheduler picks the next process.
  4. **Scenario B (Burst $> q$):** If the process's CPU burst is longer than the time quantum, the timer interrupt goes off. The OS preempts the process, context-switches, puts the preempted process at the **tail (back) of the ready queue**, and gives the CPU to the next process.

[Click here to see the Gantt Chart for Round Robin Scheduling Queue and Preemption](https://www.google.com/search?q=Round+Robin+scheduling+gantt+chart+circular+queue+diagram+OS&udm=2)

### Performance and the Choice of Time Quantum ($q$)
The performance of the RR algorithm depends heavily on the size of the time quantum.
- **If $q$ is very large:** RR behaves exactly like the FCFS policy (no preemption happens because everyone finishes within their slice).
- **If $q$ is very small:** The RR approach is called *processor sharing*. However, a very small time quantum causes a massive performance degradation because of **context switch overhead**. If $q$ is 1 ms, and a context switch takes 1 ms, the CPU spends 50% of its time doing useful work and 50% just switching!
- **Rule of Thumb:** The time quantum must be large with respect to the context switch time (usually $q$ is 10ms-100ms, and context switch is < 10 microseconds).

### Question 1 (Round Robin Calculation)
**Source:** *End Semester Examination – November 2025 (Module 2, Q3 – RR portion, 8 Marks, CO2)*

**Question:**
*Assume you have the following jobs shown in the table to execute with one processor. Calculate the average waiting time and average turnaround time if the system uses Round Robin Scheduling (time slice is 4ms).*

| Process | Burst Time(ms) | Arrival Time(ms) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| **P0** | 11 | 0 |
| **P1** | 13 | 5 |
| **P2** | 6 | 9 |
| **P3** | 9 | 13 |
| **P4** | 12 | 17 |

**Answer / Solution:**

**1. Execution Trace and Gantt Chart (Time Quantum = 4):**
*Note: When a process's time slice expires at the exact same moment a new process arrives, the new arrival is typically added to the queue first, and the preempted process is added behind it.*

- **t=0:** P0 arrives. Queue (Q): `[P0]`. CPU runs P0 (0 to 4).
- **t=4:** P0 preempted (Rem: 7). No arrivals yet. Q: `[P0]`. CPU runs P0 again (4 to 8).
- *(t=5): P1 arrives. Q: `[P1]`.*
- **t=8:** P0 preempted (Rem: 3). P0 added to tail. Q: `[P1, P0]`. CPU runs P1 (8 to 12).
- *(t=9): P2 arrives. Q: `[P0, P2]`.*
- **t=12:** P1 preempted (Rem: 9). P1 added to tail. Q: `[P0, P2, P1]`. CPU runs P0 (12 to 15).
- **t=15:** P0 finishes (only needed 3ms). *(t=13): P3 arrived.* Q at t=15 is `[P2, P1, P3]`. CPU runs P2 (15 to 19).
- *(t=17): P4 arrives. Q: `[P1, P3, P4]`.*
- **t=19:** P2 preempted (Rem: 2). P2 added to tail. Q: `[P1, P3, P4, P2]`. CPU runs P1 (19 to 23).
- **t=23:** P1 preempted (Rem: 5). P1 added to tail. Q: `[P3, P4, P2, P1]`. CPU runs P3 (23 to 27).
- **t=27:** P3 preempted (Rem: 5). P3 added to tail. Q: `[P4, P2, P1, P3]`. CPU runs P4 (27 to 31).
- **t=31:** P4 preempted (Rem: 8). P4 added to tail. Q: `[P2, P1, P3, P4]`. CPU runs P2 (31 to 33).
- **t=33:** P2 finishes (only needed 2ms). Q: `[P1, P3, P4]`. CPU runs P1 (33 to 37).
- **t=37:** P1 preempted (Rem: 1). P1 to tail. Q: `[P3, P4, P1]`. CPU runs P3 (37 to 41).
- **t=41:** P3 preempted (Rem: 1). P3 to tail. Q: `[P4, P1, P3]`. CPU runs P4 (41 to 45).
- **t=45:** P4 preempted (Rem: 4). P4 to tail. Q: `[P1, P3, P4]`. CPU runs P1 (45 to 46).
- **t=46:** P1 finishes (only needed 1ms). Q: `[P3, P4]`. CPU runs P3 (46 to 47).
- **t=47:** P3 finishes (only needed 1ms). Q: `[P4]`. CPU runs P4 (47 to 51).
- **t=51:** P4 finishes.

*Gantt Chart:*
`|P0(0-4)|P0(4-8)|P1(8-12)|P0(12-15)|P2(15-19)|P1(19-23)|P3(23-27)|P4(27-31)|P2(31-33)|P1(33-37)|P3(37-41)|P4(41-45)|P1(45-46)|P3(46-47)|P4(47-51)|`

**2. Calculation Table:**
*(CT = Completion Time, TAT = CT - AT, WT = TAT - BT)*

| Process | AT | BT | CT | TAT (CT - AT) | WT (TAT - BT) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **P0** | 0 | 11 | 15 | 15 - 0 = **15** | 15 - 11 = **4** |
| **P1** | 5 | 13 | 46 | 46 - 5 = **41** | 41 - 13 = **28** |
| **P2** | 9 | 6 | 33 | 33 - 9 = **24** | 24 - 6 = **18** |
| **P3** | 13 | 9 | 47 | 47 - 13 = **34** | 34 - 9 = **25** |
| **P4** | 17 | 12 | 51 | 51 - 17 = **34** | 34 - 12 = **22** |
| **TOTAL**| | | | **148** | **97** |

**3. Final Averages:**
- **Average Turnaround Time:** $148 / 5 =$ **29.6 ms**
- **Average Waiting Time:** $97 / 5 =$ **19.4 ms**

### Question 2 (Round Robin Quantum Comparison)
**Source:** *Internal Test – I, August 2026 (Q4a – 5 Marks, BL-3, CO2)*

**Question:**
*Four processes P1, P2, P3 and P4 arrive at time 0 with CPU burst times 8 ms, 4 ms, 2 ms and 6 ms, respectively. Apply Round Robin scheduling with time quanta of 2 ms and 4 ms. Draw the Gantt charts and analyze how the choice of time quantum affects the number of context switches and average turnaround time.*

**Answer / Solution:**

**1. Case A: Time Quantum = 2 ms**
All arrive at `t=0`. Initial queue: `[P1, P2, P3, P4]`.
- P1(0-2) $\rightarrow$ P2(2-4) $\rightarrow$ P3(4-6, **Finishes**) $\rightarrow$ P4(6-8) $\rightarrow$ P1(8-10) $\rightarrow$ P2(10-12, **Finishes**) $\rightarrow$ P4(12-14) $\rightarrow$ P1(14-16) $\rightarrow$ P4(16-18, **Finishes**) $\rightarrow$ P1(18-20, **Finishes**).

*Gantt Chart (Q=2):*
`|P1(0-2)|P2(2-4)|P3(4-6)|P4(6-8)|P1(8-10)|P2(10-12)|P4(12-14)|P1(14-16)|P4(16-18)|P1(18-20)|`
- **Completion Times:** P1=20, P2=12, P3=6, P4=18.
- **TAT (Since AT=0, TAT=CT):** P1=20, P2=12, P3=6, P4=18. 
- **Average TAT:** $(20 + 12 + 6 + 18) / 4 = 56 / 4 =$ **14 ms.**
- **Context Switches:** There are **9 context switches** between processes (at t=2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18).

**2. Case B: Time Quantum = 4 ms**
Initial queue: `[P1, P2, P3, P4]`.
- P1(0-4) $\rightarrow$ P2(4-8, **Finishes**) $\rightarrow$ P3(8-10, **Finishes**, only needed 2) $\rightarrow$ P4(10-14) $\rightarrow$ P1(14-18, **Finishes**) $\rightarrow$ P4(18-20, **Finishes**).

*Gantt Chart (Q=4):*
`|P1(0-4)|P2(4-8)|P3(8-10)|P4(10-14)|P1(14-18)|P4(18-20)|`
- **Completion Times:** P1=18, P2=8, P3=10, P4=20.
- **TAT (Since AT=0, TAT=CT):** P1=18, P2=8, P3=10, P4=20.
- **Average TAT:** $(18 + 8 + 10 + 20) / 4 = 56 / 4 =$ **14 ms.**
- **Context Switches:** There are **5 context switches** between processes (at t=4, 8, 10, 14, 18).

**3. Analysis of the Choice of Time Quantum:**
- **Context Switches:** A smaller time quantum ($q=2$) dramatically increases the number of context switches (9 switches vs 5 switches). In a real OS, this increases overhead, as CPU time is wasted saving and loading registers instead of executing processes.
- **Turnaround Time:** In this specific scenario, the Average TAT happens to be identical (14 ms) for both quanta. However, generally, a larger time quantum reduces context switch overhead and often improves turnaround time, while a smaller quantum improves *response time* (interactivity) at the cost of higher overhead.

### Quick Self-Check Key Points for Revision
- **Priority:** Run highest priority. Problem: **Starvation**. Solution: **Aging** (slowly increase priority over time).
- **Round Robin:** Time-sharing, uses a **Time Quantum ($q$)**. 
- **RR Queue Logic:** If time slice expires and a new process arrives at the same time, put the new process in the queue *first*, then put the preempted process behind it.
- **Quantum Size Effect:** $q$ too large $\rightarrow$ becomes FCFS. $q$ too small $\rightarrow$ excessive context-switch overhead slows down the whole computer.
