# Module 4.4: Weight Initialization Techniques in Neural Networks

## 1. What is Weight Initialization and Why is it Critical?
**Weight Initialization** is the procedure of setting the initial numerical values of the synaptic weight matrices ($W^{(l)}$) and bias vectors ($\mathbf{b}^{(l)}$) of a neural network **before** starting the training process (Gradient Descent and Backpropagation).

Because neural network training is a non-convex optimization problem that iteratively updates weights using:

$$
w_{\text{new}} = w_{\text{old}} - \eta \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}
$$

where the algorithm starts in the weight space determines **whether the network converges at all, how fast it converges, and the quality of the final model**.

### Three Core Objectives of Good Weight Initialization:
1. **Breaking Symmetry:** Ensuring that different neurons in the same hidden layer learn **different** features from the input data.
2. **Preventing the Vanishing Gradient Problem:** Keeping the variance of activations and backpropagated gradients from shrinking exponentially to zero ($0$) as they pass through deep layers.
3. **Preventing Exploding Gradients & Neuron Saturation:** Keeping the net inputs $z$ from growing uncontrollably large ($\to \pm\infty$), which would either push Sigmoid/Tanh neurons into their flat saturation regions ($\phi'(z) \approx 0$) or cause numerical overflow (`NaN` values).

## 2. Pitfalls of Naive Initialization Methods (Crucial for Exams!)

### Pitfall 1: Zero Initialization ($W = 0$) or Constant Initialization ($W = c$) $\longrightarrow$ The Symmetry Problem!
Suppose we initialize all weights in a neural network to **$0$** (or to the same constant value, such as $w_{ij} = 0.5$):
1. **Identical Forward Pass:** In any hidden layer, every neuron $j$ is connected to the exact same inputs $\mathbf{x}$ with the exact same weights. Therefore, every neuron in that layer computes the **exact same net input $z_1 = z_2 = \dots = z_m$** and produces the **exact same output activation $a_1 = a_2 = \dots = a_m$**.
2. **Identical Backward Pass (Identical Gradients):** During backpropagation, by symmetry, every neuron in the hidden layer receives the **exact same error gradient** $\displaystyle \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{1i}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{2i}} = \dots = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{mi}}$.
3. **Permanent Symmetry Lock:** After the weight update step, all neurons in the layer still have identical weights! Even after $10,000$ epochs of training, all neurons in a hidden layer compute the exact same function.
4. **Consequence:** A hidden layer with $500$ neurons behaves as if it has **only $1$ single neuron**, completely destroying the representational power of the neural network!
* **Rule:** **Weights must NEVER be initialized to zero or to identical constants; they must be initialized randomly to break symmetry.**
* *(Note on Biases $\mathbf{b}$: Once random weights break symmetry, **biases CAN and SHOULD be initialized to $0$**, i.e., $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$).*

---

### Pitfall 2: Random Initialization with Too-Small Weights (e.g., $w \sim N(0, 0.001^2)$)
* **What happens:** Randomness breaks symmetry, so neurons are different. However, as the signal propagates forward through $L$ layers ($a^{(l)} = \phi(W^{(l)} a^{(l-1)})$), multiplying by tiny numbers ($< 0.01$) at every layer causes activations to shrink exponentially toward **$0$**.
* During backpropagation, the gradients $\displaystyle \frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(l)}}$ also get multiplied by these tiny weights ($W^T$), causing **Vanishing Gradients**—early layers do not learn at all!

---

### Pitfall 3: Random Initialization with Too-Large Weights (e.g., $w \sim N(0, 1)$ or $N(0, 5^2)$)
* **What happens:** If a hidden neuron has $n_{\text{in}} = 500$ inputs and each weight has variance $1$, the net input $\displaystyle z = \sum_{i=1}^{500} w_i x_i$ will have a huge variance ($\text{Var}(z) = 500$)!
* **Effect on Sigmoid / Tanh Networks (Saturation):** Large $|z|$ values (e.g., $z = +25$ or $-25$) fall deep in the flat tails of Sigmoid ($\sigma(z) \approx 1$ or $0$) or Tanh ($\tanh(z) \approx \pm 1$), where the derivative **$\phi'(z) \approx 0$**. Consequently, gradients vanish immediately!
* **Effect on ReLU Networks (Exploding Gradients):** Since ReLU is unbounded for $z > 0$, multiplying by large weight matrices across $L$ layers causes activations and gradients to grow exponentially ($> 10^{30}$), resulting in **Exploding Gradients** and `NaN` overflow errors.

---

## 3. Variance-Preserving Weight Initialization Techniques (Modern Standards)

To prevent activations and gradients from either vanishing or exploding, modern initialization techniques set the **variance of the initial weights $\text{Var}(W)$** as a function of the layer sizes:
* Let **$n_{\text{in}}$ ($\text{fan}_{\text{in}}$)** $=$ Number of inputs coming *into* a neuron in layer $l$ (i.e., number of neurons in layer $l-1$).
* Let **$n_{\text{out}}$ ($\text{fan}_{\text{out}}$)** $=$ Number of outputs going *out* from the layer (i.e., number of neurons in layer $l$).

---

### Technique 1: LeCun Initialization (LeCun et al., 1998)
* **Principle:** Preserves the variance of activations during the **forward pass** ($\text{Var}(z^{(l)}) = \text{Var}(x^{(l-1)})$).
* Since $\displaystyle z = \sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{in}}} w_i x_i$, assuming zero mean and independent inputs/weights:

$$
\text{Var}(z) = n_{\text{in}} \cdot \text{Var}(w) \cdot \text{Var}(x)
$$

  To make $\text{Var}(z) = \text{Var}(x)$, we set **$\displaystyle n_{\text{in}} \cdot \text{Var}(w) = 1 \implies \text{Var}(w) = \frac{1}{n_{\text{in}}}$**.
* **Formulas:**
  * **LeCun Normal:**

$$
W \sim N\left(0, \; \sigma^2 = \dfrac{1}{n_{\text{in}}}\right)
$$

  * **LeCun Uniform:**

$$
W \sim \text{Uniform}\left(-\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{n_{\text{in}}}}, \; +\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{n_{\text{in}}}}\right)
$$

    *(Recall from Module 1.6 that a Uniform distribution on $[-r, +r]$ has variance $\displaystyle \frac{(2r)^2}{12} = \frac{r^2}{3}$. Setting $\displaystyle \frac{r^2}{3} = \frac{1}{n_{\text{in}}}$ gives $\displaystyle r = \sqrt{\frac{3}{n_{\text{in}}}}$!)*
* **Best Used With:** **SELU** (Self-Normalizing Neural Networks) and **Tanh** activations.

---

### Technique 2: Xavier / Glorot Initialization (Glorot & Bengio, 2010)
* **Principle:** LeCun initialization only preserves variance in the *forward pass* ($1/n_{\text{in}}$). To preserve gradient variance in the *backward pass*, we need $\text{Var}(w) = 1/n_{\text{out}}$.
* **Xavier (Glorot) Initialization** satisfies both directions simultaneously as a compromise by setting the weight variance to the **harmonic mean** (inverse of average of $n_{\text{in}}$ and $n_{\text{out}}$):

$$
\text{Var}(W) = \dfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}
$$

* **Formulas (Must Memorize for Exams!):**
  1. **Xavier / Glorot Normal Distribution:**

$$
W \sim N\left(0, \; \sigma^2 = \dfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}\right) \quad \left(\text{Standard Deviation } \sigma = \sqrt{\dfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}\right)
$$

  2. **Xavier / Glorot Uniform Distribution:**

$$
W \sim \text{Uniform}\left(-\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}, \; +\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}\right)
$$

* **Best Used With:** **Sigmoid ($\sigma$), Hyperbolic Tangent ($\tanh$), and Softmax** activation functions (activations that are roughly linear with unit slope near $z = 0$).

---

### Technique 3: He / Kaiming Initialization (He et al., 2015)
* **Why Xavier Fails for Deep ReLU Networks:**
  * Xavier initialization assumes that the activation function is symmetric around zero ($\phi(z) \approx z$ near $0$).
  * However, **ReLU ($\max(0, z)$) sets all negative values ($z < 0$) to exact zero**! This effectively wipes out half of the distribution at every layer, **cutting the variance of the output in half ($\displaystyle \frac{1}{2}$) at every single layer**. Over $L$ layers, variance shrinks by $(1/2)^L \to 0$!
* **Principle of He Initialization:** To compensate for ReLU halving the variance at each layer, Kaiming He et al. **doubled the variance** of LeCun initialization:

$$
\text{Var}(W) = \dfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}
$$

* **Formulas (Must Memorize for Exams!):**
  1. **He Normal Distribution:**

$$
W \sim N\left(0, \; \sigma^2 = \dfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}\right) \quad \left(\text{Standard Deviation } \sigma = \sqrt{\dfrac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}}\right)
$$

  2. **He Uniform Distribution:**

$$
W \sim \text{Uniform}\left(-\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{n_{\text{in}}}}, \; +\sqrt{\dfrac{6}{n_{\text{in}}}}\right)
$$

* **Best Used With:** **ReLU, Leaky ReLU, PReLU, and ELU** activation functions (the default choice for modern Deep Feed-Forward and Convolutional Neural Networks!).

## 5. Exam-Style Questions & Answers for Topic 4.4

### Question 1 (4 Marks)
> **Why should weights in a neural network never be initialized to zero? Explain the symmetry problem and state how biases are initialized.**

#### **Solution:**
1. **The Symmetry Problem with Zero Initialization ($W = 0$):**
   * If all weights in a neural network are initialized to $0$ (or any identical constant $c$), every neuron in a given hidden layer receives the exact same inputs multiplied by the exact same weights, producing the exact same net input $z$ and activation $a = \phi(z)$ during forward propagation.
   * Consequently, during backpropagation, every neuron in that hidden layer receives the **exact same error gradient** ($\displaystyle \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$) and undergoes the exact same weight update.
   * As a result, the neurons remain symmetric forever and learn the exact same feature—making a layer of $N$ neurons behave like a **single neuron**!
2. **Breaking Symmetry & Bias Initialization:**
   * To break symmetry, weights $W$ must always be initialized **randomly** (using techniques like Xavier or He initialization).
   * Once random weights break symmetry, **biases ($\mathbf{b}$) can safely be initialized to zero ($\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$)** because the distinct random weights already ensure each neuron computes a different linear combination.

---

### Question 2 (5 Marks)
> **Explain Xavier (Glorot) initialization and He initialization with their mathematical formulas. Which activation functions is each method suited for, and why?**

#### **Solution:**

#### **1. Xavier (Glorot) Initialization:**
* **Concept:** Proposed by Glorot and Bengio (2010) to keep the variance of activations and backpropagated gradients roughly equal across all layers of a deep network, preventing both vanishing and exploding gradients.
* **Formulas:** Let $n_{\text{in}}$ be the number of input connections and $n_{\text{out}}$ be the number of output neurons of a layer:
  * **Xavier Normal:** $\displaystyle W \sim N\left(0, \; \sigma^2 = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}\right)$
  * **Xavier Uniform:** $\displaystyle W \sim \text{Uniform}\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}, \; +\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}}}}\right)$
* **Suitability:** Best suited for **Sigmoid, Tanh, and Softmax** activation functions, because its derivation assumes the activation function is symmetric and approximately linear near zero.

#### **2. He (Kaiming) Initialization:**
* **Concept:** Proposed by Kaiming He et al. (2015) specifically for **ReLU** networks. Because $\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$ sets all negative inputs ($z < 0$) to zero, it cuts the variance of activations in half ($\displaystyle \frac{1}{2}$) at each layer. To prevent the signal variance from shrinking by $(1/2)^L$ across $L$ layers, He initialization doubles the weight variance to $\displaystyle \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}$.
* **Formulas:**
  * **He Normal:** $\displaystyle W \sim N\left(0, \; \sigma^2 = \frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}}\right)$
  * **He Uniform:** $\displaystyle W \sim \text{Uniform}\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}}}}, \; +\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{\text{in}}}}\right)$
* **Suitability:** Best suited for **ReLU, Leaky ReLU, and ELU** activation functions in deep feed-forward and convolutional neural networks.
