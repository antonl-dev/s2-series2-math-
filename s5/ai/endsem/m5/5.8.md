# Module 5.8: Unsupervised Learning – Introduction to Clustering

## 1. What is Clustering?
**Clustering** (or **Cluster Analysis**) is the most important and widely used **unsupervised machine learning** technique. 
* Given an **unlabeled dataset** $D = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N\}$ (where no target class labels $y_i$ are provided), **clustering partitions the data points into a set of meaningful groups or subsets called "Clusters" ($C_1, C_2, \dots, C_K$)** based solely on the intrinsic similarity of their features.

[Click here to see the diagram for Unsupervised Clustering Intra-Cluster Cohesion and Inter-Cluster Separation](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&q=Clustering+Unsupervised+Learning+Intra+cluster+and+Inter+cluster+distance+diagram)

---

### Two Fundamental Criteria of a Good Clustering
Every clustering algorithm aims to optimize two properties simultaneously:
1. **High Intra-Cluster Similarity (Cohesion / Compactness):**
   * Data points within the **same cluster** should be as **similar** (close in distance) to one another as possible.
2. **Low Inter-Cluster Similarity (Separation):**
   * Data points belonging to **different clusters** should be as **dissimilar** (far apart in distance) from one another as possible.

```text
         Cluster 1 (C1)                         Cluster 2 (C2)
           *   *                                  o   o
         *  [u1] *   <--- Large Inter-Cluster ---> o [u2] o
           *   *          Distance (Separation)      o   o
      (Small Intra-Cluster                       (Small Intra-Cluster
       Distance / Cohesion)                       Distance / Cohesion)
```

### Classification vs. Clustering (Quick Comparison)

| Feature | Classification (Supervised) | Clustering (Unsupervised) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Training Data** | Uses **labeled data** $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ with predefined class labels. | Uses **unlabeled data** $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}$ with no predefined classes. |
| **Prior Knowledge of Classes** | Number of classes and their definitions are known beforehand. | Number of natural groups ($K$) and their structure are discovered from data. |
| **Goal** | Predict the class label of new unseen test instances. | Group similar instances together to uncover hidden structure/segments. |
| **Examples** | SVM, Naïve Bayes, Decision Trees. | $K$-Means, Hierarchical Clustering, DBSCAN. |

## 2. Distance (Dissimilarity) Measures in Clustering
Since clustering has no labels to guide it, it relies on a **Distance Metric $d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$** to quantify how close (similar) or far apart (dissimilar) two data points $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, \dots, x_d)$ and $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \dots, y_d)$ are in the $d$-dimensional feature space:

### 1. Euclidean Distance ($L_2$ Norm — The Default Standard)
$$d_{\text{Euclidean}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{d} (x_m - y_m)^2} = \sqrt{(x_1 - y_1)^2 + (x_2 - y_2)^2 + \dots + (x_d - y_d)^2}$$

#### **Why is Euclidean Distance Most Often Used in Clustering? (Crucial for Exams!)**
1. **Intuitive Geometric Meaning (Straight-Line Distance):** It measures the true "as-the-crow-flies" straight-line physical distance between two points in Euclidean space, naturally forming compact, spherical (isotropic) clusters.
2. **Smooth Differentiability & Exact Compatibility with Cluster Centroids ($K$-Means):**
   * Squared Euclidean distance $\| \mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \|_2^2 = \sum_{m=1}^d (x_m - \mu_{km})^2$ is a **smooth, convex, and differentiable** function.
   * Because its derivative with respect to $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ is linear, the point that minimizes the sum of squared Euclidean distances to all points in a cluster is **uniquely given by the arithmetic mean (Centroid)** of those points! This makes algorithms like $K$-Means converge rapidly.
3. **Rotation and Translation Invariance:** Euclidean distance is invariant to any rigid rotation or translation of the feature axes.
4. **Satisfies All Metric Axioms & Fast Computation:** It satisfies non-negativity ($d \ge 0$), symmetry ($d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = d(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{x})$), identity ($d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}) = 0$), and the triangle inequality ($d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) \le d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) + d(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{z})$).

---

### 2. Other Distance Metrics Used in Special Cases
* **Manhattan Distance ($L_1$ / City-Block Distance):**
  $$d_{\text{Manhattan}}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{m=1}^{d} |x_m - y_m|$$
  *(More robust to outliers than Euclidean distance; used in $K$-Medians / $K$-Medoids).*
* **Minkowski Distance ($L_p$ Norm — Generalization of $L_1$ and $L_2$):**
  $$d_p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \left( \sum_{m=1}^{d} |x_m - y_m|^p \right)^{1/p}$$
  *(When $p = 1 \to$ Manhattan; when $p = 2 \to$ Euclidean; when $p \to \infty \to$ Chebyshev $\max_m |x_m - y_m|$).*
* **Cosine Similarity / Distance:** Measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors: $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|}$. Widely used in **Text / Document Clustering** where document length varies.

## 3. Major Types of Clustering Algorithms

[Click here to see the diagram for Types of Clustering Algorithms K-Means Hierarchical and DBSCAN](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&q=Types+of+Clustering+Algorithms+K+Means+Hierarchical+DBSCAN+diagram)

### A. Partitioning-Based Clustering: $K$-Means Clustering Algorithm
**$K$-Means** is the most famous clustering algorithm. It partitions $N$ data points into a user-specified number of **$K$ non-overlapping clusters**, where each cluster $C_k$ is represented by its **Centroid ($\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$)** (the mean vector of all points in $C_k$).

#### **Objective Function of $K$-Means (Within-Cluster Sum of Squares — WCSS):**
$K$-Means minimizes the sum of squared Euclidean distances between each data point and its assigned cluster centroid:
$$J = \text{WCSS} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_k} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|_2^2$$

#### **Step-by-Step $K$-Means Algorithm (Exam Must-Know!):**
1. **Step 1 (Choose $K$ & Initialize Centroids):** Specify the number of clusters $K$, and randomly select $K$ data points as the initial cluster centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \boldsymbol{\mu}_2, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_K$.
2. **Step 2 (Assignment Step):** For every data point $\mathbf{x}_i$ ($i = 1, \dots, N$), compute its **Euclidean distance** to each of the $K$ centroids and assign $\mathbf{x}_i$ to the cluster with the **nearest centroid**:
   $$c^{(i)} = \arg\min_{k \in \{1, \dots, K\}} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|_2$$
3. **Step 3 (Update / Recenter Step):** Recompute the centroid $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ of each cluster $C_k$ by taking the **arithmetic mean** of all data points currently assigned to $C_k$:
   $$\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{|C_k|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in C_k} \mathbf{x}_i$$
4. **Step 4 (Convergence Check):** Repeat **Step 2 and Step 3** until cluster assignments no longer change (or centroids stop moving).

### B. Hierarchical Clustering
Builds a multilevel hierarchy of clusters represented visually as a tree diagram called a **Dendrogram** (does not require pre-specifying $K$ upfront):
1. **Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering (Bottom-Up):** Starts by treating each of the $N$ data points as its own individual singleton cluster, and at each step merges the **two closest clusters** until all points are merged into a single root cluster.
2. **Divisive Hierarchical Clustering (Top-Down):** Starts with all $N$ points in one giant cluster and recursively splits them into smaller clusters.

[Click here to see the diagram for Hierarchical Clustering Dendrogram](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&q=Hierarchical+Clustering+Dendrogram+diagram)

---

### C. Density-Based Clustering (DBSCAN)
* **DBSCAN** (*Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise*) groups together points that are closely packed together in high-density regions, marking points that lie alone in low-density regions as **outliers/noise**.
* **Advantages over $K$-Means:** Does not require specifying $K$ in advance, can discover **arbitrarily shaped clusters** (e.g., crescents, rings), and is robust to outliers.

## 4. Real-World Applications of Clustering
1. **Customer Segmentation & Targeted Marketing:** Grouping retail or banking customers based on income, age, and purchasing history to design personalized marketing campaigns.
2. **Image Segmentation & Compression:** Grouping pixels with similar RGB color intensities to segment objects in medical scans or compress images (color quantization).
3. **Anomaly / Fraud / Intrusion Detection:** Identifying points that do not belong to any dense cluster (outliers) to flag fraudulent credit card transactions or network cyber-attacks.
4. **Document & News Grouping:** Automatically grouping thousands of daily news articles by topic (e.g., Google News) or clustering biological genes with similar expression patterns in bioinformatics.

> **Why is Euclidean distance often used in clustering?**

#### **Solution:**
Euclidean distance ($d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^d (x_i - y_i)^2}$) is the most widely used distance metric in clustering for the following reasons:
1. **Direct Geometric Intuition & Compact Spherical Clusters:** It measures the true straight-line ("as-the-crow-flies") distance between points in multidimensional space and is invariant to rotation/translation, naturally grouping points into compact, spherical clusters.
2. **Mathematical Compatibility with Cluster Centroids ($K$-Means):** Squared Euclidean distance is smooth, convex, and differentiable. Minimizing the sum of squared Euclidean distances (Within-Cluster Sum of Squares) has a simple closed-form optimum: the **arithmetic mean (centroid)** of the cluster points, making algorithms like $K$-Means fast and computationally efficient.

### Question 2 [Practice Exam Numerical on $K$-Means Clustering (5 Marks)]
> **Apply one iteration of the $K$-Means clustering algorithm to group the following 1D data points into $K = 2$ clusters: $\{2, 4, 10, 12, 3, 20, 30, 11, 25\}$. Assume initial cluster centroids are $\mu_1 = 2$ and $\mu_2 = 4$.**

#### **Solution:**

1. **Initial Centroids:** $\mu_1 = 2$, $\mu_2 = 4$.
2. **Step 1 (Assignment Step using Euclidean Distance $|x_i - \mu_k|$):**
   * For $x = 2$: $|2-2|=0 < |2-4|=2 \implies C_1$
   * For $x = 3$: $|3-2|=1 = |3-4|=1 \implies C_1$
   * For $x = 4, 10, 12, 20, 30, 11, 25$: All are closer to $\mu_2 = 4$ than to $\mu_1 = 2 \implies C_2$.
   * **Clusters after Iteration 1 Assignment:**
     * $C_1 = \{2, 3\}$
     * $C_2 = \{4, 10, 11, 12, 20, 25, 30\}$
3. **Step 2 (Update Centroids as Arithmetic Mean of Assigned Points):**
   * $\mu_1^{\text{new}} = \frac{2 + 3}{2} = \mathbf{2.5}$
   * $\mu_2^{\text{new}} = \frac{4 + 10 + 11 + 12 + 20 + 25 + 30}{7} = \frac{112}{7} = \mathbf{16.0}$
   *(In the next iteration, points $\le 9.25$ join $C_1 = \{2, 3, 4\}$ and points $> 9.25$ join $C_2 = \{10, 11, 12, 20, 25, 30\}$, rapidly converging to $\mu_1 = 7.0, \; C_1=\{2,3,4,10,11,12\}$ and $\mu_2 = 25.0, \; C_2=\{20,25,30\}$!)*
