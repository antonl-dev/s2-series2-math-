# Module 5.1: Supervised Learning – Linear Regression

## 1. What is Linear Regression?
**Linear Regression** is a foundational **supervised machine learning** and statistical algorithm used for **regression tasks**—predicting a **continuous numerical target variable $y \in \mathbb{R}$** (dependent/response variable) by modeling its linear relationship with one or more **input features $x$** (independent/explanatory/predictor variables).

## 2. Simple Linear Regression vs. Multiple Linear Regression

### A. Simple Linear Regression
Models the linear relationship between **one single independent input variable ($x$)** and a continuous dependent output variable ($y$):

$$
y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \epsilon \quad \text{(Population Model)}
$$

$$
\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x \quad \text{(Fitted Prediction Line)}
$$

* **$y$:** Actual observed target value.
* **$\hat{y}$:** Predicted target value lying on the regression line.
* **$b_0$ ($\beta_0$):** **Intercept (Bias)** — The predicted value of $\hat{y}$ when $x = 0$.
* **$b_1$ ($\beta_1$):** **Slope (Regression Coefficient / Weight)** — The expected change in $\hat{y}$ for a $1$-unit increase in $x$.
* **$\epsilon$:** Random error term (noise).

### B. Multiple Linear Regression
Models the linear relationship between **two or more independent input variables ($x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p$)** and a single continuous dependent output variable ($y$):

$$
\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \dots + b_p x_p = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b_0
$$

* Here, each coefficient $b_j$ represents the change in $\hat{y}$ per unit change in feature $x_j$ **while holding all other features $x_k \; (k \neq j)$ constant**.

### Comparison Table: Simple vs. Multiple Linear Regression

| Feature | Simple Linear Regression | Multiple Linear Regression |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Number of Independent Variables ($x$)** | **Exactly one ($1$)** predictor variable ($x$). | **Two or more ($p \ge 2$)** predictor variables ($x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p$). |
| **Mathematical Equation** | $\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x$ | $\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \dots + b_p x_p$ |
| **Geometric Representation** | A **2D straight line** in the $(x, y)$ plane. | A **3D flat plane** (for $p=2$) or a **$p$-dimensional hyperplane** in $\mathbb{R}^{p+1}$. |
| **Complexity & Risks** | Simple; no risk of collinearity between inputs. | Higher complexity; susceptible to **multicollinearity** (correlated predictors) and overfitting. |
| **Real-World Example** | Predicting student exam score ($y$) using **only** hours of study ($x$). | Predicting house price ($y$) using square footage ($x_1$), number of bedrooms ($x_2$), and age of house ($x_3$). |

## 3. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Estimation & Formulas

### A. Residuals ($e_i$) and Residual Sum of Squares ($\text{SSE}$)
For each data point $(x_i, y_i)$ in a training dataset of $n$ observations:
* **Predicted Value:** $\hat{y}_i = b_0 + b_1 x_i$
* **Residual (Prediction Error $e_i$):** The vertical signed distance between the **actual observed value $y_i$** and the **predicted value $\hat{y}_i$**:

$$
e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i
$$

  * **If $e_i > 0$ ($y_i > \hat{y}_i$):** The actual observation $(x_i, y_i)$ lies **ABOVE** the fitted regression line (the model underpredicted).
  * **If $e_i < 0$ ($y_i < \hat{y}_i$):** The actual observation $(x_i, y_i)$ lies **BELOW** the fitted regression line (the model overpredicted).
  * **If $e_i = 0$ ($y_i = \hat{y}_i$):** The observation lies **EXACTLY ON** the regression line.

* **Least-Squares Criterion:** The **Method of Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)** chooses the coefficients $b_0$ and $b_1$ that **minimize the Sum of Squared Errors (Residuals) $\text{SSE}$ (or $\text{RSS}$)**:

$$
\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - (b_0 + b_1 x_i)\big)^2
$$

---

### B. Formulas for Least-Squares Coefficients $b_1$ and $b_0$ (Must Memorize!)
By taking partial derivatives $\displaystyle \frac{\partial (\text{SSE})}{\partial b_0} = 0$ and $\displaystyle \frac{\partial (\text{SSE})}{\partial b_1} = 0$, we obtain the exact closed-form formulas:

1. **Sample Means ($\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$):**

$$
\bar{x} = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i, \qquad \bar{y} = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i
$$

2. **Slope Coefficient ($b_1$):**

$$
b_1 = \dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \dfrac{n\sum x_i y_i - \left(\sum x_i\right)\left(\sum y_i\right)}{n\sum x_i^2 - \left(\sum x_i\right)^2}
$$

3. **Intercept Coefficient ($b_0$) & The Centroid Property:**
   * **Crucial Property:** Every least-squares regression line **always passes directly through the sample mean point (centroid) $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$**!
   * Therefore, $\bar{y} = b_0 + b_1 \bar{x}$, which gives:

$$
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}
$$

   * Furthermore, the two points $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ and $(x, \hat{y})$ both lie on the line, so the slope $b_1$ can also be found from any predicted point $(x, \hat{y})$ and $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ as:

$$
b_1 = \dfrac{\hat{y} - \bar{y}}{x - \bar{x}}
$$

## 4. Goodness of Fit: Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$)
How well does the fitted linear regression line explain the observed data? We measure this using the **Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$)**:

### A. Sum of Squares Decomposition
1. **Total Sum of Squares ($\text{SST}$):** Total variation of actual $y_i$ values around their mean $\bar{y}$:

$$
\text{SST} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \bar{y})^2
$$

2. **Regression (Explained) Sum of Squares ($\text{SSR}$):** Variation explained by the regression line:

$$
\text{SSR} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 = b_1^2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = b_1 \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})
$$

3. **Error (Residual / Unexplained) Sum of Squares ($\text{SSE}$):** Variation left unexplained (residuals):

$$
\text{SSE} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
$$

   **Fundamental Identity:** $\text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE}$

### B. Formula & Interpretation of $R^2$

$$
R^2 = \dfrac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \dfrac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}, \qquad 0 \le R^2 \le 1
$$

* *(Shortcut for Simple Linear Regression: $\displaystyle R^2 = r^2 = \frac{[\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})]^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 \sum(y_i-\bar{y})^2} = \frac{b_1 \sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\text{SST}}$).*
* **Interpretation:**
  * $R^2$ represents the **proportion (or percentage) of the total variance in the dependent variable $y$ that is explained by the linear regression model on $x$**.
  * $R^2 = 1$ ($100\%$): Perfect linear fit (all points lie exactly on the line, $\text{SSE} = 0$).
  * $R^2 = 0$ ($0\%$): The linear model explains none of the variability in $y$.

## 5. Solved University & Internal Exam Questions

### Question 1 [End Semester Exam - Nov 2025, Part A, Q10 (2 Marks)]
> **Differentiate simple and multiple linear regression.**

#### **Solution:**

| Basis | Simple Linear Regression | Multiple Linear Regression |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Definition** | Models the linear relationship between **one independent variable ($x$)** and one continuous dependent variable ($y$). | Models the linear relationship between **two or more independent variables ($x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p$)** and one continuous dependent variable ($y$). |
| **2. Equation** | $\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x$ | $\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \dots + b_p x_p$ |
| **3. Geometry** | Fits a **2D straight line** to the data. | Fits a **plane (3D)** or **hyperplane ($p$-D)** to the data. |
| **4. Example** | Predicting salary ($y$) from years of experience ($x$). | Predicting house price ($y$) from area ($x_1$), rooms ($x_2$), and location score ($x_3$). |

### Question 2 [Series Test-II, Part A, Q5 (2 Marks)]
> **A least-squares line $\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 x$ is fitted to data with $\bar{x} = 4$ and $\bar{y} = 30$. The fitted model predicts $\hat{y} = 36$ at $x = 6$. Find $b_0$ and $b_1$, and the residual for the observation $(x, y) = (6, 33)$. Is this observation above or below the line?**

#### **Solution:**

**Step 1: Find Slope $b_1$ and Intercept $b_0$**
* Every least-squares regression line passes through the mean point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y}) = (4, 30)$, so:

$$
30 = b_0 + 4 b_1 \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}
$$

* We are also given that at $x = 6$, the predicted value is $\hat{y} = 36$:

$$
36 = b_0 + 6 b_1 \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}
$$

* Subtract Equation 1 from Equation 2:

$$
(36 - 30) = (6 - 4)b_1 \implies 6 = 2b_1 \implies \mathbf{b_1 = 3}
$$

* Substitute $b_1 = 3$ into Equation 1 to find $b_0$:

$$
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x} = 30 - 3(4) = 30 - 12 = \mathbf{18}
$$

  *(So the fitted line is $\hat{y} = 18 + 3x$).*

**Step 2: Find the Residual for $(x, y) = (6, 33)$ and Position Relative to the Line**
* At $x = 6$, the actual value is $y = 33$ and the predicted value on the line is $\hat{y} = 36$.
* **Residual ($e$):**

$$
e = y - \hat{y} = 33 - 36 = \mathbf{-3}
$$

* **Is the observation above or below the line?**
  Since the actual value $y = 33$ is less than the predicted value $\hat{y} = 36$ on the line (i.e., residual $e = -3 < 0$), the observation $(6, 33)$ lies **$3$ units BELOW the regression line**.

### Question 3 [Series Test-II, Part B, Q10(a) (5 Marks)]
> **The hours of practice $x$ and the score $y$ of five students in a programming test are:**
> 
> | $x$ (hours) | $2$ | $4$ | $6$ | $8$ | $10$ |
> | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> | $y$ (score) | $12$ | $16$ | $21$ | $24$ | $27$ |
> 
> **(i) Find $\bar{x}, \bar{y}$ and the least-squares coefficients $b_1$ and $b_0$.**  
> **(ii) Predict the score for $x = 12$.**  
> **(iii) Calculate $R^2$. Interpret the value of $R^2$.**

#### **Solution:**

#### **Part (i): Find $\bar{x}, \bar{y}$ and Least-Squares Coefficients $b_1$ and $b_0$**

**1. Calculate Sample Means ($\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$) for $n = 5$:**

$$
\bar{x} = \dfrac{2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10}{5} = \dfrac{30}{5} = \mathbf{6}
$$

$$
\bar{y} = \dfrac{12 + 16 + 21 + 24 + 27}{5} = \dfrac{100}{5} = \mathbf{20}
$$

**2. Construct Deviation Table for Least-Squares Calculation:**

| Student $i$ | $x_i$ | $y_i$ | $x_i - \bar{x}$ | $y_i - \bar{y}$ | $(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})$ | $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$ | $(y_i - \bar{y})^2$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **1** | $2$ | $12$ | $-4$ | $-8$ | $+32$ | $16$ | $64$ |
| **2** | $4$ | $16$ | $-2$ | $-4$ | $+8$ | $4$ | $16$ |
| **3** | $6$ | $21$ | $0$ | $+1$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1$ |
| **4** | $8$ | $24$ | $+2$ | $+4$ | $+8$ | $4$ | $16$ |
| **5** | $10$ | $27$ | $+4$ | $+7$ | $+28$ | $16$ | $49$ |
| **Total ($\displaystyle \sum$)** | **$30$** | **$100$** | **$0$** | **$0$** | **$S_{xy} = \mathbf{76}$** | **$S_{xx} = \mathbf{40}$** | **$\text{SST} = \mathbf{146}$** |

**3. Compute Slope $b_1$ and Intercept $b_0$:**

$$
b_1 = \dfrac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \dfrac{76}{40} = \mathbf{1.9}
$$

$$
b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x} = 20 - (1.9 \times 6) = 20 - 11.4 = \mathbf{8.6}
$$

✅ **Fitted Least-Squares Regression Equation:**

$$
\mathbf{\hat{y} = 8.6 + 1.9x}
$$

---

#### **Part (ii): Predict the Score for $x = 12$ Hours**
Substitute $x = 12$ into the fitted equation $\hat{y} = 8.6 + 1.9x$:

$$
\hat{y}(12) = 8.6 + 1.9(12) = 8.6 + 22.8 = \mathbf{31.4}
$$

---

#### **Part (iii): Calculate $R^2$ and Interpret Its Value**

**Method 1 (Direct Sum of Squares Formula):**
* Total Sum of Squares: $\displaystyle \text{SST} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = \mathbf{146}$
* Explained (Regression) Sum of Squares:

$$
\text{SSR} = b_1 \sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y}) = 1.9 \times 76 = \mathbf{144.4}
$$

  *(Or checking each predicted value $\hat{y}_i = 8.6 + 1.9x_i$: at $x = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10$, we get $\hat{y} = 12.4, 16.2, 20.0, 23.8, 27.6$; the residuals $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ are $-0.4, -0.2, +1.0, +0.2, -0.6$; so $\text{SSE} = (-0.4)^2 + (-0.2)^2 + (1.0)^2 + (0.2)^2 + (-0.6)^2 = 0.16 + 0.04 + 1.00 + 0.04 + 0.36 = \mathbf{1.6}$. Indeed, $\text{SSR} = \text{SST} - \text{SSE} = 146 - 1.6 = 144.4$!)*

* **Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$):**

$$
R^2 = 1 - \dfrac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \dfrac{1.6}{146} = \dfrac{144.4}{146} \approx \mathbf{0.9890} \quad (\text{or } \mathbf{98.90\%})
$$

**Interpretation of $R^2 = 0.9890$:**
* **$98.90\%$ of the total variation in the students' programming test scores ($y$) is explained by the linear relationship with their hours of practice ($x$)**, and only $1.10\%$ of the variation is due to unexplained random factors/residuals.
* Since $R^2 = 0.989$ is extremely close to $1$, the least-squares line $\hat{y} = 8.6 + 1.9x$ provides an **excellent linear fit** to the data.
