# Module 1.1: Bayes' Theorem and Random Variables

## Part 1: Probability Foundations & Bayes' Theorem

### 1. Basic Terminology in Probability
Before diving into Bayes' Theorem, let us review the foundational building blocks of probability theory:
* **Random Experiment:** An experiment or process for which the outcome cannot be predicted with certainty beforehand (e.g., tossing a coin, testing a patient for a disease, predicting whether an email is spam).
* **Sample Space ($S$ or $\Omega$):** The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment.
* **Event ($E$ or $A, B$):** Any subset of the sample space.
* **Probability of an Event $P(A)$:** A numerical measure of the likelihood that event $A$ will occur, always satisfying:

$$
0 \le P(A) \le 1 \quad \text{and} \quad P(S) = 1
$$

---

### 2. Joint, Marginal, and Conditional Probability

#### a) Joint Probability $P(A \cap B)$ or $P(A, B)$
The probability that **both** events $A$ and $B$ occur simultaneously.
* **Independent Events:** Two events $A$ and $B$ are statistically independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)
$$

#### b) Conditional Probability $P(A \mid B)$
The probability that event $A$ occurs **given that** event $B$ has already occurred.

$$
P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad \text{provided } P(B) > 0
$$

Rearranging this gives the **Multiplication Rule of Probability**:

$$
P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)
$$

### 3. Law of Total Probability
Suppose events $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ form a **partition** of the sample space $S$ (meaning they are mutually exclusive, $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$, and collectively exhaustive, $\displaystyle \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i = S$). 

Then, for any event $B$ in $S$, the total probability of $B$ can be found by summing the probabilities of $B$ occurring alongside each partition $A_i$:

$$
P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B \mid A_i) \cdot P(A_i)
$$

$$
P(B) = P(B \mid A_1)P(A_1) + P(B \mid A_2)P(A_2) + \dots + P(B \mid A_n)P(A_n)
$$

[Click here to see the diagram for Law of Total Probability Partition Diagram](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&q=Law+of+Total+Probability+Partition+Venn+Diagram)

---

### 4. Bayes' Theorem

#### Statement
If $A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n$ are mutually exclusive and exhaustive events that partition the sample space $S$ (with $P(A_i) > 0$ for all $i$), and $B$ is any event with $P(B) > 0$, then the conditional probability of $A_k$ given that event $B$ has occurred is given by:

$$
P(A_k \mid B) = \dfrac{P(B \mid A_k) \cdot P(A_k)}{P(B)} = \dfrac{P(B \mid A_k) \cdot P(A_k)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B \mid A_i) \cdot P(A_i)}
$$

For a simple binary case (Hypothesis $H$ and Evidence $E$):

$$
P(H \mid E) = \dfrac{P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E)} = \dfrac{P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E \mid H)P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)P(\neg H)}
$$

#### Mathematical Proof / Derivation
1. By the definition of conditional probability:

$$
P(A_k \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A_k \cap B)}{P(B)} \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}
$$

and

$$
P(B \mid A_k) = \dfrac{P(B \cap A_k)}{P(A_k)} \implies P(A_k \cap B) = P(B \mid A_k) \cdot P(A_k) \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}
$$

2. Substitute Equation 2 into the numerator of Equation 1:

$$
P(A_k \mid B) = \dfrac{P(B \mid A_k) \cdot P(A_k)}{P(B)}
$$

3. Expanding the denominator $P(B)$ using the **Law of Total Probability**:

$$
P(A_k \mid B) = \dfrac{P(B \mid A_k) \cdot P(A_k)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B \mid A_i) \cdot P(A_i)}
$$

### 5. AI & Machine Learning Interpretation of Bayes' Theorem
In Artificial Intelligence, Bayes' theorem is used to **update our belief** about a hypothesis when new evidence arrives:

$$
\text{Posterior} = \dfrac{\text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}}{\text{Evidence (Marginal Likelihood)}}
$$

| Term | Mathematical Symbol | Meaning in AI / Machine Learning |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Prior Probability** | $P(H)$ or $P(A_k)$ | Initial belief about the hypothesis *before* observing any new evidence (e.g., baseline disease prevalence in a population). |
| **Likelihood** | $P(E \mid H)$ or $P(B \mid A_k)$ | Probability of observing the evidence $E$ given that the hypothesis $H$ is true (e.g., sensitivity/accuracy of a medical test). |
| **Evidence (Marginal)** | $P(E)$ or $P(B)$ | Total probability of observing the evidence under all possible hypotheses (acts as a normalizing constant). |
| **Posterior Probability** | $P(H \mid E)$ or $P(A_k \mid B)$ | Updated belief about the hypothesis *after* taking the observed evidence into account. |

#### Applications of Bayes' Theorem in AI:
1. **Medical Diagnosis Systems:** Computing the true probability that a patient has a disease given a positive diagnostic test result (accounting for false positives and disease rarity).
2. **Spam Filtering (Naïve Bayes Classifier):** Calculating the probability that an email is spam given the words it contains.
3. **Robotic Localization & Sensor Fusion:** Updating a robot's belief about its position based on noisy sensor readings (Kalman Filters, Particle Filters).
4. **Fault Diagnosis in Engineering:** Identifying the root cause of a machine failure given observed symptoms/alarms.

## Part 2: Random Variables

### 1. Definition of a Random Variable
A **Random Variable ($X$)** is a real-valued function that assigns a unique numerical value to each outcome in the sample space $S$ of a random experiment.

Mathematically:

$$
X : S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}
$$

* **Crucial Note:** Despite its name, a random variable is **neither random nor a variable** in the algebraic sense—it is a **deterministic function** that maps qualitative or raw experimental outcomes ($\omega \in S$) onto the real number line ($\mathbb{R}$). The "randomness" comes from the underlying experiment whose outcome is uncertain.

[Click here to see the diagram for Random Variable mapping from Sample Space to Real Line](https://www.google.com/search?udm=2&q=Random+Variable+mapping+sample+space+to+real+line)

---

### 2. Notation Convention
* **Capital letters ($X, Y, Z$):** Denote the random variable itself (the function/rule).
* **Lowercase letters ($x, y, z$):** Denote a specific numerical value (realization) that the random variable can take.
* Example: $P(X = x)$ means *"the probability that the random variable $X$ takes the specific numerical value $x$"*.

---

### 3. Why Do We Need Random Variables in AI & Data Science?
Real-world outcomes are often qualitative or categorical (e.g., `{Rain, No Rain}`, `{Product A, Product B, No Purchase}`, `{Heads, Tails}`). Mathematical models, statistical formulas (like Mean and Variance), and Machine Learning algorithms can only operate on **numbers**. Random variables bridge this gap by **encoding outcomes numerically**.

#### Illustrative Examples:
1. **Coin Toss Experiment:**
   * Experiment: Toss 2 coins simultaneously.
   * Sample Space: $S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$
   * Define Random Variable $X =$ *"Number of Heads obtained"*.
   * Mapping:
     * $X(TT) = 0$
     * $X(HT) = 1, \quad X(TH) = 1$
     * $X(HH) = 2$
   * Range (State Space) of $X$: $R_X = \{0, 1, 2\}$.

2. **Customer Purchase Prediction (AI Encoding Example):**
   * Suppose a customer can either buy `Product A`, buy `Product B`, or make `No Purchase`.
   * Since outcomes are categorical, we define a random variable $X$ to encode them into numbers (e.g., based on profit, utility, or numerical labels):
     * $X(\text{No Purchase}) = 0$
     * $X(\text{Product B}) = 1$
     * $X(\text{Product A}) = 2$
   * Once encoded as a random variable $X \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, we can compute expected value (mean), variance, and probability distributions.

---

### 4. Types of Random Variables (Brief Overview)
*(Covered in detail in Module 1.2)*
1. **Discrete Random Variable:** Takes a finite or countably infinite number of distinct values (typically counts, like $0, 1, 2, \dots$).
2. **Continuous Random Variable:** Can take any value within one or more intervals on the real number line (typically measurements, like height, temperature, rainfall amount, time).

### Question 1 [End Semester Exam - Nov 2025, Q12 (8 Marks)]
> **a) A person is moving from house to office using three ways: by car, train, and bus. Probability of all three are equal. Probability of getting late if he is traveling by car is $0.3$, by bus is $0.4$, by train is $0.1$. Now it is found that he is late today. What is the probability that he is traveling by using the bus? (5 Marks)**  
> **b) Also find the probability of traveling by car and train. (3 Marks)**

#### **Solution:**

**Step 1: Define the Events**
* Let $C =$ Event that the person travels by **Car**
* Let $B =$ Event that the person travels by **Bus**
* Let $T =$ Event that the person travels by **Train**
* Let $L =$ Event that the person is **Late** to the office

**Step 2: Write the Given Prior Probabilities**
Since the probabilities of all three modes of transport are equal and they form a partition of the sample space:

$$
P(C) = P(B) = P(T) = \dfrac{1}{3}
$$

**Step 3: Write the Given Conditional Probabilities (Likelihoods)**
* Probability of getting late given Car: $P(L \mid C) = 0.3$
* Probability of getting late given Bus: $P(L \mid B) = 0.4$
* Probability of getting late given Train: $P(L \mid T) = 0.1$

---

#### **Part (a): Probability that he traveled by Bus given he is Late, $P(B \mid L)$**

By **Law of Total Probability**, the total probability of being late $P(L)$ is:

$$
P(L) = P(L \mid C)P(C) + P(L \mid B)P(B) + P(L \mid T)P(T)
$$

$$
P(L) = \left(0.3 \times \dfrac{1}{3}\right) + \left(0.4 \times \dfrac{1}{3}\right) + \left(0.1 \times \dfrac{1}{3}\right)
$$

$$
P(L) = \dfrac{0.3 + 0.4 + 0.1}{3} = \dfrac{0.8}{3}
$$

By **Bayes' Theorem**, the probability that he traveled by Bus given that he is late today is:

$$
P(B \mid L) = \dfrac{P(L \mid B) \cdot P(B)}{P(L)}
$$

$$
P(B \mid L) = \dfrac{0.4 \times \dfrac{1}{3}}{\dfrac{0.8}{3}} = \dfrac{0.4}{0.8} = \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.5
$$

✅ **Answer (a):** The probability that he is traveling by bus given he is late is **$0.5$** (or **$50\%$**).

---

#### **Part (b): Probability of traveling by Car and Train (given he is late)**

**1. Probability that he traveled by Car given he is late, $P(C \mid L)$:**

$$
P(C \mid L) = \dfrac{P(L \mid C) \cdot P(C)}{P(L)}
$$

$$
P(C \mid L) = \dfrac{0.3 \times \dfrac{1}{3}}{\dfrac{0.8}{3}} = \dfrac{0.3}{0.8} = \dfrac{3}{8} = 0.375
$$

**2. Probability that he traveled by Train given he is late, $P(T \mid L)$:**

$$
P(T \mid L) = \dfrac{P(L \mid T) \cdot P(T)}{P(L)}
$$

$$
P(T \mid L) = \dfrac{0.1 \times \dfrac{1}{3}}{\dfrac{0.8}{3}} = \dfrac{0.1}{0.8} = \dfrac{1}{8} = 0.125
$$

*(Verification Check: $P(B \mid L) + P(C \mid L) + P(T \mid L) = 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.125 = 1.0$. Correct!)*

✅ **Answer (b):** 
* Posterior probability of traveling by **Car** = **$0.375$** ($37.5\%$)
* Posterior probability of traveling by **Train** = **$0.125$** ($12.5\%$)

### Question 2 [Series Test-I, Q7(a) (5 Marks)]
> **A hospital uses an AI system to detect a disease.**
> * **Disease prevalence = $2\%$**
> * **Test accuracy for diseased patients = $95\%$**
> * **False positive rate = $4\%$**
> 
> **A patient tests positive. Find the probability that the patient actually has the disease and explain why Bayes' theorem is important in medical AI.**

#### **Solution:**

**Step 1: Define the Events**
* Let $D =$ Event that the patient **actually has the disease**
* Let $\neg D$ (or $D'$) $=$ Event that the patient **does not have the disease** (healthy)
* Let $+$ $=$ Event that the AI test result is **Positive**

**Step 2: Write the Given Probabilities**
* **Prior Probability of Disease (Prevalence):**

$$
P(D) = 2\% = 0.02
$$

* **Prior Probability of No Disease:**

$$
P(\neg D) = 1 - P(D) = 1 - 0.02 = 0.98
$$

* **True Positive Rate (Sensitivity / Accuracy for diseased patients):**

$$
P(+ \mid D) = 95\% = 0.95
$$

* **False Positive Rate (Probability of testing positive when actually healthy):**

$$
P(+ \mid \neg D) = 4\% = 0.04
$$

**Step 3: Apply Bayes' Theorem**
We need to find the posterior probability $P(D \mid +)$:

$$
P(D \mid +) = \dfrac{P(+ \mid D) \cdot P(D)}{P(+)}
$$

First, calculate total probability of a positive test $P(+)$ using the Law of Total Probability:

$$
P(+) = P(+ \mid D)P(D) + P(+ \mid \neg D)P(\neg D)
$$

$$
P(+) = (0.95 \times 0.02) + (0.04 \times 0.98)
$$

$$
P(+) = 0.019 + 0.0392 = 0.0582
$$

Now substitute into Bayes' formula:

$$
P(D \mid +) = \dfrac{0.019}{0.0582} \approx 0.3265 \quad (\text{or } 32.65\%)
$$

**Step 4: Why Bayes' Theorem is Important in Medical AI**
1. **Prevents the Base-Rate Fallacy:** Even though the test looks highly accurate ($95\%$ sensitivity and only $4\%$ false positive rate), a positive result only means a **$32.65\%$** chance of actually having the disease because the disease is rare ($2\%$ prevalence). Without Bayes' theorem, doctors or patients might mistakenly assume a positive test means a $95\%$ chance of having the disease.
2. **Incorporates Prior Clinical Knowledge:** It combines population-level prevalence/risk factors (Prior) with diagnostic test results (Likelihood) to produce calibrated risk probabilities (Posterior).
3. **Reduces Unnecessary Invasive Procedures:** By showing that false positives outnumber true positives when screening for rare diseases, medical AI systems know to recommend a follow-up confirmatory test before starting aggressive treatment.

### Question 3 [Series Test-I, Q11(a) - Random Variable Encoding Concept (4 Marks)]
> **An AI predicts customer purchases with probabilities: Product A: $0.60$, Product B: $0.25$, No purchase: $0.15$. Encode the outcomes suitably and calculate: Mean & Variance. Explain how the variance can help a marketing manager.**  
> *(Note: Full Mean & Variance theory is covered in 1.5, but we solve it here as well since it directly demonstrates encoding qualitative outcomes into a Random Variable $X$.)*

#### **Solution:**

**Step 1: Define and Encode the Random Variable ($X$)**
The experimental outcomes $S = \{\text{No purchase}, \text{Product B}, \text{Product A}\}$ are categorical. We define a discrete random variable $X$ by assigning numerical codes to each outcome:
* Let $X = 0$ for **No purchase** $\implies P(X = 0) = 0.15$
* Let $X = 1$ for **Product B** $\implies P(X = 1) = 0.25$
* Let $X = 2$ for **Product A** $\implies P(X = 2) = 0.60$

*(Note: Alternatively, one can encode $X=1$ for Product A, $X=2$ for Product B, $X=0$ for No purchase; both are completely valid in exams as long as the encoding is clearly stated! Let's also show the $X(\text{Product A})=1, X(\text{Product B})=2, X(\text{No Purchase})=0$ encoding below so you have both).*

**Using Encoding 1: $X(\text{No purchase}) = 0, \; X(\text{Product B}) = 1, \; X(\text{Product A}) = 2$**
* **Mean $E(X) = \mu$:**

$$
\mu = \sum x_i P(X = x_i) = (0 \times 0.15) + (1 \times 0.25) + (2 \times 0.60) = 0 + 0.25 + 1.20 = 1.45
$$

* **Expected Square $E(X^2)$:**

$$
E(X^2) = \sum x_i^2 P(X = x_i) = (0^2 \times 0.15) + (1^2 \times 0.25) + (2^2 \times 0.60) = 0 + 0.25 + 2.40 = 2.65
$$

* **Variance $\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2$:**

$$
\sigma^2 = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2 = 2.65 - (1.45)^2 = 2.65 - 2.1025 = 0.5475
$$

**Using Encoding 2: $X(\text{Product A}) = 1, \; X(\text{Product B}) = 2, \; X(\text{No purchase}) = 0$**
* **Mean $E(X)$:**

$$
\mu = (1 \times 0.60) + (2 \times 0.25) + (0 \times 0.15) = 0.60 + 0.50 + 0 = 1.10
$$

* **Expected Square $E(X^2)$:**

$$
E(X^2) = (1^2 \times 0.60) + (2^2 \times 0.25) + (0^2 \times 0.15) = 0.60 + 1.00 + 0 = 1.60
$$

* **Variance $\text{Var}(X)$:**

$$
\sigma^2 = 1.60 - (1.10)^2 = 1.60 - 1.21 = 0.39
$$

**Step 2: How Variance Helps a Marketing Manager**
* **Measures Purchase Uncertainty / Volatility:** Variance quantifies how spread out or unpredictable customer purchasing behavior is around the expected average.
* **Inventory & Campaign Planning:** A **low variance** indicates consistent, predictable customer preference (mostly buying Product A), allowing lean inventory management. A **high variance** indicates volatile buying patterns, signaling that the marketing manager needs targeted promotions and safety stock to handle demand fluctuations.
