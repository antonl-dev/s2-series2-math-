# Module 1.7: Normal Distribution (Gaussian Distribution)

## 1. Introduction & Definition
The **Normal Distribution**—also known as the **Gaussian Distribution** (after Carl Friedrich Gauss)—is the most important and widely used **continuous probability distribution** in statistics, engineering, and Artificial Intelligence.

A continuous random variable $X$ taking real values in $(-\infty, \infty)$ is said to follow a **Normal Distribution** with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, denoted as:
$$X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$$
if its **Probability Density Function (PDF)** is given by:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \, \exp\left( -\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \quad -\infty < x < \infty$$

### Parameters of the Normal Distribution:
1. **$\mu$ (Mean — Location Parameter):**$-\infty < \mu < \infty$. It determines the **center (peak)** of the bell curve. Changing $\mu$ shifts the entire curve horizontally left or right without changing its shape.
2. **$\sigma$ (Standard Deviation — Scale/Spread Parameter):** $\sigma > 0$ (where $\sigma^2$ is the variance). It determines the **width (spread) and height** of the bell curve:
   * **Small $\sigma$:** Tall, narrow, tightly concentrated curve around $\mu$.
   * **Large $\sigma$:** Short, flat, widely spread curve.
3. **Constants:** $\pi \approx 3.14159$, $e \approx 2.71828$, and $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.3989$.

## 2. Key Properties of the Normal Distribution (Crucial for Exams)

1. **Bell-Shaped Curve:** The graph of $f(x)$ is a smooth, continuous, single-peaked (**unimodal**) bell-shaped curve.
2. **Perfect Symmetry (Zero Skewness):** The curve is **perfectly symmetrical** about the vertical line $x = \mu$:
   $$f(\mu + c) = f(\mu - c) \quad \text{for any } c$$
   Consequently, $50\%$ of the area lies to the left of $\mu$ and $50\%$ lies to the right of $\mu$:
   $$P(X \le \mu) = P(X \ge \mu) = 0.5$$
3. **Coincidence of Mean, Median, and Mode:** Because the distribution is unimodal and perfectly symmetrical about its center:
   $$\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode} = \mu$$
4. **Maximum Density at $x = \mu$:** The peak (highest value) of the PDF occurs at $x = \mu$ (where the exponent becomes $0$ and $e^0 = 1$):
   $$f_{\max} = f(\mu) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx \frac{0.3989}{\sigma}$$
5. **Asymptotic to the Horizontal Axis:** Both tails extend indefinitely from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ and approach the $x$-axis ($f(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$) without ever touching or crossing it.
6. **Total Area Under the Curve Equals $1$:**
   $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\,dx = 1$$
7. **Points of Inflection:** The curve changes concavity (from concave downward to concave upward) at one standard deviation away from the mean on either side:
   $$x = \mu - \sigma \quad \text{and} \quad x = \mu + \sigma$$

## 3. The Empirical Rule ($68\text{–}95\text{–}99.7\%$ Rule)
For any normal distribution $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the percentage of values that lie within $1, 2,$ and $3$ standard deviations of the mean is fixed:
* **$\mu \pm 1\sigma$:** Contains approximately **$68.27\%$** (or $\approx 68\%$) of the data:
  $$P(\mu - \sigma \le X \le \mu + \sigma) \approx 0.6827$$
* **$\mu \pm 2\sigma$:** Contains approximately **$95.45\%$** (or $\approx 95\%$) of the data:
  $$P(\mu - 2\sigma \le X \le \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 0.9545$$
* **$\mu \pm 3\sigma$:** Contains approximately **$99.73\%$** (or $\approx 99.7\%$) of the data:
  $$P(\mu - 3\sigma \le X \le \mu + 3\sigma) \approx 0.9973$$

## 4. Standard Normal Distribution ($Z$-Distribution)

### Definition
A Normal distribution with **Mean $\mu = 0$** and **Standard Deviation $\sigma = 1$** (Variance $\sigma^2 = 1$) is called the **Standard Normal Distribution**, denoted by:
$$Z \sim N(0, 1)$$

### Standardization ($Z$-Score Transformation)
Any normal random variable $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ can be converted into a Standard Normal random variable $Z \sim N(0, 1)$ using the **$Z$-transformation**:
$$Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$$
* **Interpretation of $Z$-score:** $Z$ measures **how many standard deviations** a value $x$ lies above ($Z > 0$) or below ($Z < 0$) the mean $\mu$.
* **Standard Normal PDF $\phi(z)$:**
  $$\phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{z^2}{2}}, \quad -\infty < z < \infty$$
* **Relation between $f(x)$ and $\phi(z)$:**
  $$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma} \phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sigma} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{z^2}{2}} \right)$$

## 5. Why is the Normal Distribution So Important in AI & Data Science?
1. **Central Limit Theorem (CLT):** The sum or average of a large number of independent random variables (regardless of their original distribution) converges to a Normal distribution. This explains why natural phenomena (heights, student marks, measurement errors) follow a bell curve.
2. **Feature Scaling (Z-Score Standardization):** In Machine Learning (e.g., SVM, Linear Regression, Neural Networks), input features are standardized using $z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ so they have zero mean and unit variance.
3. **Gaussian Naïve Bayes:** Continuous features in Naïve Bayes classification are modeled by assuming each class conditional distribution $P(X_i \mid Y)$ follows a Normal distribution.
4. **Noise Modeling:** Sensor noise in robotics and error terms ($\epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$) in Linear Regression are modeled using Gaussian distributions.

## 6. Solved University & Internal Exam Questions

### Question 1 [End Semester Exam - Nov 2025, Part A, Q2 (2 Marks)]
> **A dataset shows that its mean, median, and mode are all equal. What type of distribution is this likely to be, and what does this imply about its shape?**

#### **Solution:**
1. **Type of Distribution:** The dataset is likely to follow a **Normal Distribution (Gaussian Distribution)**.
2. **Implications about its Shape:**
   * **Perfectly Symmetrical (Zero Skewness):** The left half of the distribution is an exact mirror image of the right half around the central value ($\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode} = \mu$). There is neither positive (right) skew nor negative (left) skew.
   * **Unimodal Bell-Shaped Curve:** It has a single central peak at the mode/mean, and the frequencies taper off equally and smoothly toward both tails.

### Question 2 [Series Test-I, Part B, Q8(b) (5 Marks)] & [Series Test-I, Part B, Q11(b) (4 Marks)]
> **Q8(b): Marks of students follow a normal distribution. Mean $= 70$ & Standard deviation $= 10$. Find the probability density at $x = 80$. Explain what this value represents.**  
> *(Note: Q11(b) asks the exact same calculation written in notation: "Student marks follow $N(70, 10^2)$. Find the probability density at $x = 80$.")*

#### **Solution:**

**Step 1: Identify the Given Parameters**
The student marks $X$ follow a normal distribution $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2) = N(70, 10^2)$:
* Mean ($\mu$) $= 70$
* Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) $= 10$ (Variance $\sigma^2 = 100$)
* Point of evaluation ($x$) $= 80$

**Step 2: Write the Normal Probability Density Function (PDF) Formula**
$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}$$

**Step 3: Substitute $\mu = 70, \; \sigma = 10, \; x = 80$**
First, compute the standardized $z$-score in the exponent:
$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} = \frac{80 - 70}{10} = \frac{10}{10} = 1$$

Now substitute into the PDF formula:
$$f(80) = \frac{1}{10 \sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{1}{2}(1)^2} = \frac{1}{10 \sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-0.5}$$

**Step 4: Evaluate the Numerical Value**
* $\sqrt{2\pi} \approx \sqrt{2 \times 3.14159} = \sqrt{6.28318} \approx 2.5066$
* $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.39894$
* $e^{-0.5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{e}} \approx 0.60653$

$$f(80) = \frac{1}{10} \times 0.39894 \times 0.60653 = \frac{0.24197}{10} \approx \mathbf{0.0242}$$

**Step 5: Explain What This Value ($f(80) \approx 0.0242$) Represents**
1. **Probability Density (Rate of Probability per Unit Mark), NOT Exact Point Probability:**
   Since $X$ is a continuous random variable, the probability of a student scoring *exactly* $80.000\dots$ is $P(X = 80) = 0$. The value $f(80) = 0.0242$ is the **height of the normal bell curve** at $x = 80$, representing the **concentration of probability per unit mark** around $80$.
2. **Approximate Probability for a Small Interval Around $80$:**
   It tells us that for a small interval of width $\Delta x$ around $80$, the probability that a randomly selected student scores in that range is approximately $f(80) \times \Delta x$. For example, the probability of scoring within a $1$-mark band around $80$ (say, between $79.5$ and $80.5$, where $\Delta x = 1$) is approximately **$0.0242$ (or $2.42\%$)**.
3. **Relative Likelihood:**
   Since $x = 80$ is one standard deviation above the mean ($z = +1$), the density at $80$ ($0.0242$) is lower than the peak density at the mean $\mu = 70$ ($f(70) = \frac{1}{10\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.0399$), showing that scoring around $70$ is more common than scoring around $80$.
