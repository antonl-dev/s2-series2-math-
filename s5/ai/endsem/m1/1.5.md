# Module 1.5: Mean and Variance of Random Variables

## 1. Mean (Mathematical Expectation / Expected Value)

### Definition
The **Mean** (or **Expected Value** or **Mathematical Expectation**) of a random variable $X$, denoted by $E(X)$ or $\mu$ or $\mu_X$, is the **probability-weighted average** of all possible values that the random variable can take.

* **Physical Intuition:** It represents the **center of gravity (balance point)** of the probability distribution.
* **Statistical / AI Intuition:** If we repeat a random experiment infinitely many times, the arithmetic average of the observed outcomes converges to $E(X)$ (by the *Law of Large Numbers*).

### Formulas for Mean $\mu = E(X)$

#### 1. For a Discrete Random Variable:
If $X$ takes discrete values $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ with probabilities $P(X = x_i) = p(x_i)$:

$$
\mu = E(X) = \sum_{i} x_i \cdot P(X = x_i)
$$

#### 2. For a Continuous Random Variable:
If $X$ has a Probability Density Function (PDF) $f(x)$:

$$
\mu = E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot f(x)\,dx
$$

---

## 2. Expectation of a Function of a Random Variable $g(X)$
If $g(X)$ is any real-valued function of $X$ (for example, $g(X) = X^2$), we do not need to find the probability distribution of $g(X)$ first; we can compute $E[g(X)]$ directly:
* **Discrete Case:**

$$
E[g(X)] = \sum_{i} g(x_i) \cdot P(X = x_i)
$$

* **Continuous Case:**

$$
E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x) \cdot f(x)\,dx
$$

**Special Case ($g(X) = X^2$ — Second Raw Moment, needed for Variance):**
* **Discrete:** $\displaystyle E(X^2) = \sum_{i} x_i^2 \cdot P(X = x_i)$
* **Continuous:** $\displaystyle E(X^2) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 \cdot f(x)\,dx$

---

## 3. Properties of Mathematical Expectation (Linearity of Expectation)
Let $X$ and $Y$ be random variables, and let $a, b, c$ be real constants:
1. **Expectation of a Constant:**

$$
E(c) = c
$$

2. **Scalar Multiplication:**

$$
E(aX) = a \cdot E(X)
$$

3. **Linear Transformation:**

$$
E(aX + b) = a \cdot E(X) + b
$$

4. **Sum of Two Random Variables (Always true, whether $X, Y$ are independent or not):**

$$
E(X + Y) = E(X) + E(Y)
$$

5. **Product of Two Independent Random Variables:**
   If $X$ and $Y$ are statistically independent:

$$
E(XY) = E(X) \cdot E(Y)
$$

---

## 4. Variance and Standard Deviation

### A. Definition of Variance ($\sigma^2$ or $\text{Var}(X)$)
While the Mean ($\mu$) tells us where the center of a distribution is located, it gives no information about how spread out the values are. 

The **Variance** of a random variable $X$, denoted by $\text{Var}(X)$ or $\sigma^2$ or $V(X)$, measures the **expected squared deviation** of $X$ from its mean $\mu$:

$$
\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X) = E\left[(X - \mu)^2\right]
$$

* **Discrete Definition Formula:**

$$
\text{Var}(X) = \sum_{i} (x_i - \mu)^2 \cdot P(X = x_i)
$$

* **Continuous Definition Formula:**

$$
\text{Var}(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^2 \cdot f(x)\,dx
$$

---

### B. Computational (Shortcut) Formula for Variance & Its Proof
In exams, computing $\text{Var}(X)$ using $(x_i - \mu)^2$ is tedious when $\mu$ is a decimal or fraction. Instead, we always use the **Computational Formula**:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2 = E(X^2) - \mu^2
$$

#### **Short Proof (Often asked as a 2-mark derivation):**
Start with the definition of variance, where $\mu = E(X)$ is a constant:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E\left[(X - \mu)^2\right]
$$

Expand the square inside the expectation:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E\left[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2\right]
$$

Apply the linearity of expectation:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - 2\mu E(X) + E(\mu^2)
$$

Since $\mu = E(X)$ is a constant, $E(\mu^2) = \mu^2$:

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - 2\mu(\mu) + \mu^2 = E(X^2) - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2
$$

$$
\text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - \mu^2 = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2
$$

---

### C. Standard Deviation ($\sigma$)
Because Variance squares the deviations, its units are the **square** of the original units of $X$ (e.g., $\text{mm}^2$, $\text{dollars}^2$). To bring the measure of spread back to the same unit as the random variable $X$ and the mean $\mu$, we take the positive square root of the variance, called the **Standard Deviation ($\sigma$)**:

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)} = \sqrt{E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2}
$$

---

### D. Algebraic Properties of Variance
Let $X, Y$ be random variables and $a, b, c$ be real constants:
1. **Non-Negativity:** Variance can never be negative:

$$
\text{Var}(X) \ge 0
$$

2. **Variance of a Constant is Zero:** (Since a constant never varies):

$$
\text{Var}(c) = 0
$$

3. **Shifting by a Constant $b$ Does NOT Change Variance:**

$$
\text{Var}(X + b) = \text{Var}(X)
$$

   *(Adding a constant shifts the entire distribution left or right without changing its spread).*
4. **Scaling by a Constant $a$ Multiplies Variance by $a^2$:**

$$
\text{Var}(aX) = a^2 \cdot \text{Var}(X)
$$

5. **General Linear Transformation:**

$$
\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \cdot \text{Var}(X)
$$

$$
\sigma_{aX+b} = |a| \cdot \sigma_X
$$

6. **Sum/Difference of Independent Random Variables:**
   If $X$ and $Y$ are independent:

$$
\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)
$$

$$
\text{Var}(X - Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y)
$$

---

## 5. Summary Table of Formulas

| Metric | Symbol | Discrete Random Variable Formula | Continuous Random Variable Formula |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |

| **Second Raw Moment** | $E(X^2)$ | $\displaystyle \sum x_i^2 P(X = x_i)$ | $\displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 f(x)\,dx$ |

## 6. Solved University & Internal Exam Questions

### Question 1 [Series Test-I, Part B, Q8(a) (5 Marks)]
> **The probability distribution of a random variable is:**
> 
> | $X$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ |
> | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
> | $P(X)$ | $0.2$ | $0.4$ | $0.3$ | $0.1$ |
> 
> **Find: i) Mean $\quad$ ii) Variance $\quad$ iii) Standard deviation. Interpret the result.**

#### **Solution:**

Let us construct the computation table for $x_i P(x_i)$ and $x_i^2 P(x_i)$:

| $x_i$ | $P(X = x_i)$ | $x_i \cdot P(X = x_i)$ | $x_i^2$ | $x_i^2 \cdot P(X = x_i)$ |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $0$ | $0.2$ | $0 \times 0.2 = 0.0$ | $0$ | $0 \times 0.2 = 0.0$ |
| $1$ | $0.4$ | $1 \times 0.4 = 0.4$ | $1$ | $1 \times 0.4 = 0.4$ |
| $2$ | $0.3$ | $2 \times 0.3 = 0.6$ | $4$ | $4 \times 0.3 = 1.2$ |
| $3$ | $0.1$ | $3 \times 0.1 = 0.3$ | $9$ | $9 \times 0.1 = 0.9$ |
| **Total ($\displaystyle \sum$)** | **$\displaystyle \sum P(x_i) = 1.0$** | **$\displaystyle \sum x_i P(x_i) = 1.3$** | — | **$\displaystyle \sum x_i^2 P(x_i) = 2.5$** |

#### **i) Mean ($\mu$):**

$$
\mu = E(X) = \sum x_i P(X = x_i) = 0 + 0.4 + 0.6 + 0.3 = \mathbf{1.3}
$$

#### **ii) Variance ($\sigma^2$):**
First, from the table, $\displaystyle E(X^2) = \sum x_i^2 P(X = x_i) = 2.5$.

$$
\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X) = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2 = 2.5 - (1.3)^2 = 2.5 - 1.69 = \mathbf{0.81}
$$

#### **iii) Standard Deviation ($\sigma$):**

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}(X)} = \sqrt{0.81} = \mathbf{0.9}
$$

#### **Interpretation of the Result:**
1. **Mean ($\mu = 1.3$):** Over a large number of repeated trials of this experiment, the long-run average outcome is **$1.3$**, which lies between $1$ and $2$ (closest to $1$, which also has the highest probability of $0.4$).
2. **Variance ($\sigma^2 = 0.81$) & Standard Deviation ($\sigma = 0.9$):** On average, individual outcomes of $X$ deviate from the mean ($1.3$) by about **$0.9$ units**. The majority of the probability mass ($90\%$) lies within roughly one standard deviation of the mean, i.e., in $[1.3 - 0.9, 1.3 + 0.9] = [0.4, 2.2]$ (which covers $X = 1$ and $X = 2$).

### Question 2 [Series Test-I, Part B, Q7(b) (5 Marks)]
> **The probability density function of a continuous random variable is:**
>
> $$
> f(x) = kx, \quad 0 \le x \le 4
> $$
>
> **Find: i) The value of $k$ $\quad$ ii) Mean $\quad$ iii) Variance**

#### **Solution:**

#### **i) Value of $k$:**
Using the normalization condition $\displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$:

$$
\int_{0}^{4} kx\,dx = 1 \implies k \left[\dfrac{x^2}{2}\right]_{0}^{4} = 1 \implies 8k = 1 \implies \mathbf{k = \dfrac{1}{8}}
$$

#### **ii) Mean ($\mu$):**

$$
\mu = E(X) = \int_{0}^{4} x \cdot f(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{4} x \left(\dfrac{x}{8}\right)\,dx = \dfrac{1}{8} \left[\dfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_{0}^{4} = \dfrac{64}{24} = \mathbf{\dfrac{8}{3} \approx 2.67}
$$

#### **iii) Variance ($\sigma^2$):**
First, compute $E(X^2)$:

$$
E(X^2) = \int_{0}^{4} x^2 \cdot f(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{4} x^2 \left(\dfrac{x}{8}\right)\,dx = \dfrac{1}{8} \left[\dfrac{x^4}{4}\right]_{0}^{4} = \dfrac{256}{32} = 8
$$

Now apply the variance formula:

$$
\sigma^2 = E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2 = 8 - \left(\dfrac{8}{3}\right)^2 = 8 - \dfrac{64}{9} = \mathbf{\dfrac{8}{9} \approx 0.889}
$$

### Question 3 [Series Test-I, Part B, Q11(a) (4 Marks)]
> **An AI predicts customer purchases with probabilities: Product A: $0.60$, Product B: $0.25$, No purchase: $0.15$. Encode the outcomes suitably and calculate: Mean & Variance. Explain how the variance can help a marketing manager.**

#### **Solution:**

**Step 1: Suitable Numerical Encoding of Outcomes**
Let the discrete random variable $X$ represent the encoded customer action:
* $X = 0 \rightarrow \text{No purchase}$, with $P(X = 0) = 0.15$
* $X = 1 \rightarrow \text{Product B}$, with $P(X = 1) = 0.25$
* $X = 2 \rightarrow \text{Product A}$, with $P(X = 2) = 0.60$

*(Note: If you encode $\text{Product A}=1, \text{Product B}=2, \text{No purchase}=0$, then $\mu = 1.10$ and $\sigma^2 = 0.39$, which is also completely valid).*

**Step 2: Calculate Mean $\mu = E(X)$**

$$
\mu = E(X) = \sum x_i P(X = x_i) = (0 \times 0.15) + (1 \times 0.25) + (2 \times 0.60)
$$

$$
\mu = 0 + 0.25 + 1.20 = \mathbf{1.45}
$$

**Step 3: Calculate Variance $\sigma^2 = \text{Var}(X)$**

$$
E(X^2) = \sum x_i^2 P(X = x_i) = (0^2 \times 0.15) + (1^2 \times 0.25) + (2^2 \times 0.60) = 0 + 0.25 + 2.40 = 2.65
$$

$$
\sigma^2 = E(X^2) - \mu^2 = 2.65 - (1.45)^2 = 2.65 - 2.1025 = \mathbf{0.5475}
$$

**Step 4: How Variance Helps a Marketing Manager**
1. **Quantifying Demand Uncertainty:** Variance measures the dispersion/volatility in customer purchasing decisions.
2. **Inventory & Budget Allocation:** A **low variance** tells the marketing manager that customer choices are tightly clustered around the preferred product (Product A), enabling confident stocking and focused marketing. A **high variance** indicates high unpredictability in customer behavior, requiring diversified stock buffers and targeted promotional campaigns to convert undecided ("No purchase") customers.
