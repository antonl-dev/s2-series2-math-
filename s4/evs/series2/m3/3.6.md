# 3.6 Wastewater Disposal and Oxygen Sag Curve

## 1. Wastewater Disposal Methods
After treatment, wastewater must be disposed of safely into the environment. The two primary methods are:

### I. Disposal by Dilution
Untreated or partially treated sewage is discharged into large natural water bodies where it is purified by the **self-purification capacity** of the water.
*   **Receiving Waters:** Creeks, estuaries, groundwater, lakes, oceans, and perennial rivers.
*   **Favorable Conditions:**
    *   Large volume of diluting water.
    *   High Dissolved Oxygen (DO) content in the water.
    *   Swift currents to prevent local nuisance or sludge settling.
    *   No nearby water supply intakes or residential habitations downstream.

### II. Disposal by Land Treatment (Sewage Farming)
Sewage is applied to land where it evaporates or percolates through the soil.
*   **Benefits:** Adds fertilizing value to the land; crops can be grown profitably.
*   **Sewage Sickness:** A condition where the soil pores become clogged due to continuous application of sewage, leading to anaerobic conditions and foul smells. It can be prevented by giving the land rest, rotating crops, and providing primary treatment before application.

## 2. Self-Purification of Rivers
When sewage is discharged into a river, the concentration of pollutants decreases over time and distance due to natural processes like sedimentation, oxidation, and reduction. This process is divided into four zones:
1.  **Zone of Degradation:** Just below the outfall; water becomes dark and turbid; DO starts to decrease.
2.  **Zone of Active Decomposition:** Heavy pollution; DO may drop to zero; anaerobic conditions; fish disappear.
3.  **Zone of Recovery:** DO starts to rise again; organic matter is mostly stabilized; microscopic life reappears.
4.  **Zone of Clearer Water:** River returns to its original clean state.

---

## 3. The Oxygen Sag Curve (Streeter-Phelps)
The **Oxygen Sag Curve** is a graphical representation of the Dissolved Oxygen (DO) level in a stream over time/distance following the discharge of organic waste. It is the result of two simultaneous processes:

1.  **Deoxygenation:** Microorganisms consume DO while decomposing organic matter (BOD).
2.  **Reoxygenation:** Oxygen from the atmosphere is absorbed into the water at the surface to replenish the lost DO.

### Key Points on the Curve:
*   **Initial Deficit ($D_0$):** The difference between saturation DO and actual DO at the point of waste discharge.
*   **Critical Point:** The point where the DO level is at its **minimum**. At this point, the rate of deoxygenation equals the rate of reoxygenation.
*   **Critical Deficit ($D_c$):** The maximum oxygen deficit (lowest DO level) reached on the curve.

## 4. Streeter-Phelps Equation
This equation is used to calculate the oxygen deficit ($D_t$) at any time $t$ downstream:

$$D_t = \frac{k' L_0}{R' - k'} (10^{-k't} - 10^{-R't}) + D_0 10^{-R't}$$

**Where:**
*   $L_0$ = Initial BOD of the mix at the discharge point.
*   $k'$ = Deoxygenation constant.
*   $R'$ = Reoxygenation constant.
*   $t$ = Time of flow in days.

---

## 5. First Stage BOD
The rate of deoxygenation is assumed to be directly proportional to the amount of organic matter remaining in the sewage.

*   **BOD Exerted ($y_t$):** The amount of oxygen consumed up to time $t$.
    $$y_t = L_0 (1 - 10^{-k't})$$
*   **Temperature Effect:** The rate constant ($k$) increases with temperature, meaning oxygen is depleted faster in warmer weather.
    $$k_T = k_{20} \theta^{(T-20)}$$

---

## 6. Self-Purification Constant ($f$)
The ratio of the reoxygenation constant to the deoxygenation constant is called the self-purification constant.
$$f = \frac{R'}{k'}$$
*   A higher $f$ value indicates a faster recovery of the river's health.
